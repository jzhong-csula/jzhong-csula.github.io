What this unit is for: a better model

  • A model is a rule that predicts. Unit 2 (L04): quiz points \(y\) from hours studied \(t\), \[\hat y=w_0+w_1t .\]
  • The two numbers \(w_0,w_1\) are the model’s parameters, \(\mathbf w=(w_0,w_1)^\top\).
  • Choosing the parameters is choosing the model.
  • How good is a model? L04’s mean squared error, the average squared residual: \[\mathrm{MSE}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat y_i)^2 .\]
  • Better model = smaller error.

L09 — From One Slope to Many

  • Reading: MML §5.2, pp.146–148 (Definition 5.5, partial derivatives, p.146).

The question of this lecture:
  • the error now depends on two parameters — how does it change when we change one of them?
  • what must be true at the lowest point?
Big steps of the lecture: recall the one-variable derivative; one parameter (the best slope is L03's projection fraction); two parameters (a surface, partial derivatives, one per column of X); at a lowest point every partial derivative is zero; setting them to zero is L04's normal equation.

Recall: the derivative measures how the error changes

Unit 2’s first example: three friends guess your age, \(19,\ 21,\ 20\).

  • A single answer \(x\) has error \[f(x)=(19-x)^2+(21-x)^2+(20-x)^2 .\]
  • Derivative = change in error per unit change in the parameter (MML Def. 5.2, p.141): \[f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.\]
  • Here: \[f'(x)=-2(19-x)-2(21-x)-2(20-x)=6x-120 .\]
"Three facts, nothing more from Calculus 1." This slide is fact 1: what the derivative is.

Why SSE, not L04’s MSE?

The error we are working with: \(\mathrm{SSE}(w)=5w^2-14w+14\), best \(w^\ast=\tfrac75\).

  • SSE avoids the thirds.

Why \(w^\ast\) is L03’s projection fraction — for any data

The error we are working with: \(\mathrm{SSE}(w)=(\mathbf t^\top\mathbf t)\,w^2-2\,(\mathbf t^\top\mathbf y)\,w+\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y\).

  • Step 2 — differentiate, with residual \(\mathbf r=\mathbf y-w\mathbf t\): \[\mathrm{SSE}'(w)=2(\mathbf t^\top\mathbf t)\,w-2\,\mathbf t^\top\mathbf y=-2\,\mathbf t^\top(\mathbf y-w\mathbf t)=-2\,\mathbf t^\top\mathbf r .\]
  • Step 3 — set it to zero (\(\mathbf t\neq\mathbf 0\)), for every dataset: \[w^\ast=\dfrac{\mathbf t^\top\mathbf y}{\mathbf t^\top\mathbf t}.\qquad\text{Ours: }\tfrac75 .\]
This is L03’s projection formula \(\lambda=\dfrac{\mathbf b^\top\mathbf x}{\mathbf b^\top\mathbf b}\) with \(\mathbf b=\mathbf t\), \(\mathbf x=\mathbf y\).
  • \(\mathbf t^\top\mathbf r=0\) is L03’s picture: at the best \(w\), the residual is perpendicular to \(\mathbf t\).
"Calculus (set the derivative to zero) lands on the geometry of L03 (projection onto t), as an identity for any data."

The partial derivative

Definition (MML Def. 5.5, p.146). The partial derivative of \(g(w_0,w_1)\) with respect to \(w_0\) is \[\frac{\partial g}{\partial w_0}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{g(w_0+h,\;w_1)-g(w_0,\;w_1)}{h},\] the ordinary derivative of \(w_0\mapsto g(w_0,w_1)\) with \(w_1\) held fixed; similarly for \(w_1\). [MASTER]
  • How to compute it: treat the other parameter as a constant.
  • Then use Calculus 1.
"The definition is the Calculus 1 limit with one coordinate moving and the other nailed down."

Theorem: at a lowest point, every partial derivative of SSE is zero

Theorem. Let \(\mathrm{SSE}\) be differentiable. If it is smallest at an interior point \((w_0^\ast,w_1^\ast)^\top\) (here the parameters range over all of \(\R^2\)), then both partial derivatives are zero there. [MASTER]

Plan of the proof — restrict to a line and use the one-variable fact:

  1. Freeze \(w_1=w_1^\ast\): a function of \(w_0\) alone.
  2. That function is smallest at \(w_0^\ast\).
  3. Calculus 1: its derivative is \(0\) there — and that derivative is \(\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}/\partial w_0\).
  4. Same for \(w_1\), freezing \(w_0\) instead.
"A lowest point on the surface is still a lowest point on every line through it." The plan is the recall slide's third fact, used once per parameter.

Proof: freeze one parameter, use the one-variable fact

To prove: \(\mathrm{SSE}\) smallest at interior \((w_0^\ast,w_1^\ast)^\top\) ⇒ both partial derivatives are \(0\) there.

  1. Freeze \(w_1=w_1^\ast\): \(u(w_0)=\mathrm{SSE}(w_0,w_1^\ast)\) is a function of one variable.
  2. \(u\) is smallest at \(w_0^\ast\):
    • \(\mathrm{SSE}(w_0^\ast,w_1^\ast)\le\mathrm{SSE}(w_0,w_1)\) for every pair;
    • in particular for the pairs \((w_0,w_1^\ast)^\top\): \(u(w_0^\ast)\le u(w_0)\).
  3. Calculus 1:
    • \(w_0^\ast\) is interior to \(\R\), so \(u'(w_0^\ast)=0\).
    • \(u'\) is the derivative in \(w_0\) with \(w_1\) held at \(w_1^\ast\) (Def. 5.5), so \(\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}/\partial w_0\,(w_0^\ast,w_1^\ast)=0\).
  4. The other one: freeze \(w_0=w_0^\ast\) instead; the same steps give \(\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}/\partial w_1\,(w_0^\ast,w_1^\ast)=0\).
Ours: u'(w0) = 6w0 + 6w1* − 12, the partial derivative at w1 = w1*. The theorem goes from 'lowest' to 'flat', not back.

Theorem: the flat point is a global minimum

\[\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\norm{\mathbf y-X\mathbf w}^2,\quad \mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}1\\3\\2\end{bmatrix},\quad X=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf w_{\text{flat}}=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac32\\[2pt]\tfrac12\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=\mathbf y-X\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}=\begin{bmatrix}-\tfrac12\\1\\-\tfrac12\end{bmatrix}\]
Theorem (Pythagoras for least squares). The slope in every direction, from the two partial derivatives

Formula. If \(g\) is differentiable at \(\mathbf w\),
then for every unit direction \(\mathbf u=(u_0,u_1)^\top\), with the partial derivatives taken at \(\mathbf w\): \[D_{\mathbf u}g=u_0\,\frac{\partial g}{\partial w_0}+u_1\,\frac{\partial g}{\partial w_1}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial w_0}&\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial w_1}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf u .\] [MASTER]

Why, for our \(\mathrm{SSE}\): the plan, four steps

  1. \(\phi(h)\) is a quadratic in \(h\).
  2. So \(\phi'(0)\) is the coefficient of \(h\).
  3. Collect the \(h\)-terms.
  4. Group them by \(u_0\) and \(u_1\): the two partial derivatives appear.

Why the formula holds for our \(\mathrm{SSE}\)

The error: \(\mathrm{SSE}=3w_0^2+6w_0w_1+5w_1^2-12w_0-14w_1+14\); any point \(\mathbf w\), any unit \(\mathbf u\).

  1. \(\phi(h)=\mathrm{SSE}(w_0+hu_0,\,w_1+hu_1)=ah^2+bh+c\)  — \(\mathrm{SSE}\) is quadratic
  2. \(\phi'(0)=b\), the coefficient of \(h\)  — the \(h^2\) terms drop out at \(h=0\)
  3. The \(h\)-terms, piece by piece  — expand
    piece of \(\mathrm{SSE}\)\(3w_0^2\)\(6w_0w_1\)\(5w_1^2\)\(-12w_0-14w_1\)
    its \(h\)-coefficient\(6w_0u_0\)\(6w_0u_1+6w_1u_0\)\(10w_1u_1\)\(-12u_0-14u_1\)
  4. Group by \(u_0\) and \(u_1\): \[\phi'(0)=u_0\,\underbrace{(6w_0+6w_1-12)}_{\partial\mathrm{SSE}/\partial w_0}+\;u_1\,\underbrace{(6w_0+10w_1-14)}_{\partial\mathrm{SSE}/\partial w_1}.\]
"This is a real proof for our error, and it uses nothing but expanding brackets. The h^2 terms never matter at h = 0 — that is the whole reason the answer is linear in u."

One row answers every direction: the gradient

Definition (MML eq. (5.40), p.146). For \(g(\mathbf w)\) with \(\mathbf w\in\R^d\),

Why the gradient is defined this way

\(\nabla g=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial w_0}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial w_{d-1}}\end{bmatrix}\), and for a unit direction \(\mathbf u\): \(\;D_{\mathbf u}g=\nabla g\,\mathbf u\).

  • \(\nabla g\,\mathbf u\) is a dot product: \((\nabla g)^\top\) with \(\mathbf u\).
  • With \(\norm{\mathbf u}=1\), a dot product is a projection (L03): how far along \(\mathbf u\) the gradient’s shadow lands.
  • So: rate of change of \(g\) along \(\mathbf u\) = projection of the gradient onto \(\mathbf u\).
  • The definition is not arbitrary.
    It collects exactly the numbers whose projection gives the slope in every direction.

The gradient is a row; a direction is a column

\(\nabla g=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial w_0}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial w_{d-1}}\end{bmatrix}\in\R^{1\times d}\), \(\;\mathbf u\in\R^{d}\) a column, \(\;D_{\mathbf u}g=\nabla g\,\mathbf u\).

  • Why a row? A function with one output has a one-row derivative.
  • In L14 this row is the Jacobian of a one-output function, and the chain rule becomes a matrix product (MML Remark “Gradient as a Row Vector”, p.147).
  • ⚠️ Mind the transpose. A direction to walk in is a column: \((\nabla g)^\top\).
  • This course writes the transpose out every time.

Why \(-(\nabla g)^\top\) is always the steepest way down

  • To prove: if \(\nabla g\neq\mathbf 0\), \(D_{\mathbf u}g\) is largest along \((\nabla g)^\top\) and smallest along \(-(\nabla g)^\top\).
  • Plan: \(D_{\mathbf u}g\) is a dot product; L01’s cosine formula leaves only the angle free.
  • \(\theta\) = the angle between the columns \((\nabla g)^\top\) and \(\mathbf u\), with \(\norm{\mathbf u}=1\).

Proof

  1. \(D_{\mathbf u}g=\nabla g\,\mathbf u\) is the dot product of \((\nabla g)^\top\) and \(\mathbf u\)  — row times column
  2. \(D_{\mathbf u}g=\norm{(\nabla g)^\top}\,\norm{\mathbf u}\cos\theta\)  — L01: \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{\mathbf x^\top\mathbf y}{\norm{\mathbf x}\norm{\mathbf y}}\)
  3. \(D_{\mathbf u}g=\norm{(\nabla g)^\top}\cos\theta\)  — \(\norm{\mathbf u}=1\)
  4. \(-\norm{(\nabla g)^\top}\le D_{\mathbf u}g\le\norm{(\nabla g)^\top}\)  — \(-1\le\cos\theta\le1\)
"Only the angle is free: the length of the gradient is fixed by the point, the length of u is fixed at 1. So choosing a direction is choosing theta." "The cosine formula needs both vectors nonzero; that is why nabla g != 0 is assumed."

Step 1: write \(\mathrm{SSE}\) as a product of matrices

  • Sizes: \(n\) data points, \(d\) parameters.
  • \(X\) is \(n\times d\), \(\;\mathbf w\) is \(d\times1\), \(\;\mathbf y\) is \(n\times1\). Ours: \(n=3\), \(d=2\).
\[\begin{aligned} \mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)&=\norm{\mathbf y-X\mathbf w}^2=(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)^\top(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)&&\text{(1) squared length = dot with itself}\\ &=(\mathbf y^\top-\mathbf w^\top X^\top)(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)&&\text{(2) a transpose reverses a product}\\ &=\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y-\mathbf y^\top X\mathbf w-\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y+\mathbf w^\top X^\top X\mathbf w&&\text{(3) multiply out} \end{aligned}\]
  • In (2): \((X\mathbf w)^\top=\mathbf w^\top X^\top\).
  • In (3), keep every product in its order: matrices do not commute.
  • Each of the four products is \(1\times1\): a single number.

Step 1, key step: the two middle products are equal

From (3): \(\;\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y-\mathbf y^\top X\mathbf w-\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y+\mathbf w^\top X^\top X\mathbf w\).

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbf y^\top X\mathbf w&=(\mathbf y^\top X\mathbf w)^\top&&\text{a }1\times1\text{ matrix equals its own transpose}\\ &=\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y&&(ABC)^\top=C^\top B^\top A^\top \end{aligned}\]
  • So the two middle terms combine: \(\;-\mathbf y^\top X\mathbf w-\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y=-2\,\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y\).
\[\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\underbrace{\mathbf w^\top X^\top X\mathbf w}_{\text{quadratic in }\mathbf w}\;-\;\underbrace{2\,\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y}_{\text{linear in }\mathbf w}\;+\;\underbrace{\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y}_{\text{no }\mathbf w}\] [MASTER]

Step 2: differentiate with respect to the vector \(\mathbf w\)

L11, MML (5.40), p.146: for a number \(f(\mathbf w)\) computed from \(\mathbf w\) (L11 called it \(g\)), the gradient is the row

\[\nabla f=\frac{d f}{d\mathbf w}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial w_0}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial w_{d-1}}\end{bmatrix}.\]
  • Notation, as in L11: \(d\) for the whole vector \(\mathbf w\) (\(\tfrac{df}{d\mathbf w}\), a row); \(\partial\) for one entry (\(\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial w_j}\), a number).
  • MML §5.5 writes its rules with \(\partial f/\partial\mathbf x\) for the same row. Here they are written with \(d\).
  • Each of L12’s rules is proved one partial derivative at a time.

The two rules: Calculus 1 ideas, with one difference

Differentiate with respect to \(\mathbf w\in\R^d\): the row of partial derivatives (MML (5.40), p.146). With \(d=1\) it is the Calculus 1 derivative.

one parameter \(w\) (Calculus 1)vector \(\mathbf w\in\R^d\) (this lecture)

quadratic\((3w^2)'=3\cdot2\,w=6w\);  \((aw^2)'=2aw\)\(d(\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w)/d\mathbf w=2\,\mathbf w^\top A\), \(A\) symmetric (Rule 2)

  • ⚠️ The difference: matrix products do not commute. Keep the order, and check the sizes.
  • So the answers are rows: \(\mathbf b^\top\) (not \(\mathbf b\)); \(\;2\,\mathbf w^\top A\) (not \(2A\mathbf w\)).

Step 2: Rule 1 for the linear piece \(\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w\)

Rule 1 (MML (5.105), p.158). For a fixed column \(\mathbf b\in\R^d\): \(\;\dfrac{d(\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w)}{d\mathbf w}=\mathbf b^\top\). [MASTER]

Calculus 1: \((2w)'=2\), \((bw)'=b\). Proof, one partial derivative at a time:

  1. \(\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w=b_0w_0+b_1w_1+\cdots+b_{d-1}w_{d-1}\)  — write out the dot product.
  2. \(\partial(\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w)/\partial w_j=b_j\)  — every other term is a constant.
  3. The row of these: \(\begin{bmatrix}b_0&\cdots&b_{d-1}\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf b^\top\) ✓

On our data, \(\mathbf b=(6,7)^\top\):

  • The piece \(-2\,\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w=-12w_0-14w_1\) contributes \(\begin{bmatrix}-12&-14\end{bmatrix}\).
  • These are the constant terms of \(\nabla\mathrm{SSE}=\begin{bmatrix}6w_0+6w_1-12&6w_0+10w_1-14\end{bmatrix}\).

Rule 2 (MML (5.107), p.158: for any square \(B\) the derivative of \(\mathbf w^\top B\mathbf w\) is \(\mathbf w^\top(B+B^\top)\)).
For a symmetric \(d\times d\) matrix \(A\) (\(A^\top=A\)): \(\;\dfrac{d(\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w)}{d\mathbf w}=2\,\mathbf w^\top A\). [MASTER]
  • Calculus 1: \((3w^2)'=3\cdot2\,w=6w\). The \(2\) in Rule 2 plays the same role. Where it comes from: next.

Rule 2: where the \(2\) comes from

Rule 2: \(\;d(\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w)/d\mathbf w=2\,\mathbf w^\top A\), \(A\) symmetric.

  • \(\mathbf w\) appears on both sides of \(A\).
  • Varying the column \(\mathbf w\) on the right gives \(\mathbf w^\top A\).
  • Varying the row \(\mathbf w^\top\) on the left gives \(\mathbf w^\top A^\top\).
  • Symmetry makes them equal: \(\;\mathbf w^\top A+\mathbf w^\top A^\top=2\,\mathbf w^\top A\).
  • For \(d\) parameters: the same computation, longer; MML (5.107).
"Do not say 'each parameter appears twice'. The reason for the 2 is that w sits on both sides of A, and symmetry makes the two contributions equal. For a non-symmetric B the two contributions are w^T B and w^T B^T, which is MML (5.107)."

Rule 2: the proof for two parameters

  • To prove: \(\dfrac{d(\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w)}{d\mathbf w}=2\,\mathbf w^\top A\) for \(A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\\beta&\gamma\end{smallmatrix}\right]\), \(\mathbf w=(w_0,w_1)^\top\).
  • Plan: multiply out → take each partial derivative → recognise the row as \(\mathbf w^\top\) times \(A\).
\[\begin{aligned} \mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w&=\alpha w_0^2+2\beta\,w_0w_1+\gamma w_1^2&&\text{(1) multiply out}\\ \frac{d(\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w)}{d\mathbf w}&=\begin{bmatrix}2\alpha w_0+2\beta w_1&2\beta w_0+2\gamma w_1\end{bmatrix}&&\text{(2) one partial derivative per entry}\\ &=2\begin{bmatrix}w_0&w_1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\\beta&\gamma\end{bmatrix}=2\,\mathbf w^\top A\ ✓&&\text{(3) a row times a matrix} \end{aligned}\]
  • In (1), one \(\beta w_0w_1\) comes from the row \(\mathbf w^\top\) on the left of \(A\), the other from the column \(\mathbf w\) on its right.

  • \(\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w-2\,\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w+\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y\),  \(A=X^\top X\) (symmetric), \(\mathbf b=X^\top\mathbf y\).

Step 1: why split the error into stages

  • A neural network is a chain of layers: \(f_1\), then \(f_2\), then \(f_3\), …
  • Each layer is a function: a linear map, then a nonlinear function.
  • Forward: compute the values, layer by layer, up to the error.
  • Backward: compute the derivatives, layer by layer, back to the parameters — the chain rule. This is backpropagation.
  • Such a chain cannot be expanded into one polynomial the way L12 expanded \(\mathrm{SSE}\).
We need the derivative from the stages, one at a time. Start with the smallest chain.
"Same three students, same one-parameter model as L09. The only new thing is that we stop jumping from w straight to the error and look at every stage in between."

Step 3: the chain rule — rates multiply along a chain

Chain rule (MML eq. (5.32), p.145). If \(v=f(w)\) and \(z=g(v)\), so that \(z=g(f(w))\), then \[\frac{dz}{dw}=g'(f(w))\,f'(w),\qquad\text{written also as}\qquad \frac{dz}{dw}=\frac{dz}{dv}\cdot\frac{dv}{dw},\] with \(\frac{dz}{dv}\) evaluated at \(v=f(w)\). [MASTER]
"MML p.148 warns in the margin: dz/dv times dv/dw looks like fractions cancelling, but a derivative is not a fraction. The notation is a memory aid; the first-order argument (next slide) is the reason."

Step 3: why the chain rule holds (first order)

Two stages: \(v=f(w)\), then \(z=g(v)\). Follow a small step \(\delta\) in \(w\) through both.

  1. \(\Delta v\approx f'(w)\,\delta\) — stage 1, L09’s first-order prediction
  2. \(\Delta z\approx g'(v)\,\Delta v\) — stage 2, the same prediction
  3. \(\Delta z\approx g'(v)\,f'(w)\,\delta\) — put step 1 into step 2
  • Each stage multiplies the change by its own rate.
  • This is the first-order reason; the formal proof is in any calculus text.

The trade-off of depth: vanishing gradients

Product of \(50\) stage derivatives of about \(0.9\): about \(0.005\).

  • More layers: a more flexible model (and a greater risk of overfitting).
  • But: a parameter in an early layer gets its derivative through every later layer.
    • A long product of factors below \(1\) is nearly \(0\).
    • So that parameter barely moves: its learning is very slow.
  • This is the vanishing-gradient problem of deep networks. [UNDERSTAND]

A preview of the fix: keep every factor near \(1\) [EXPLORE]

The trouble is a long product of factors below \(1\). Deep networks are built so the factors stay near \(1\):

  • ReLU nonlinearity, \(\max(0,x)\): where it is active its slope is exactly \(1\).
  • Skip connections: a layer computes \(x+f(x)\) instead of \(f(x)\). Its derivative is \(1+f'(x)\), which stays near \(1\) when \(f'(x)\) is small.
  • Both appear in the networks behind today’s AI systems. Not on quizzes or exams.

The Jacobian: every partial derivative, in one matrix

Definition (MML Def. 5.6, (5.58)–(5.59), p.150). For \(\mathbf f:\R^n\to\R^m\) with outputs \(f_1,\dots,f_m\) and inputs \(x_1,\dots,x_n\), the Jacobian \(J_{\mathbf f}\), also written \(d\mathbf f/d\mathbf x\), is the \(m\times n\) matrix \[J_{\mathbf f}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n}\\ \vdots&&\vdots\\ \dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n}\end{bmatrix},\qquad (i,j)\text{ entry }=\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}:\] one row per output, one column per input. [MASTER]
"MML calls this arrangement numerator layout (Remark, p.150). Say 'one row per output, one column per input' twice; everything later depends on it."

Reading a Jacobian

Working with: \(J_{\mathbf f}\) is \(m\times n\), \((i,j)\) entry \(=\partial f_i/\partial x_j\).

  • Each entry is a partial derivative (L09, MML Def. 5.5).
  • Each entry is a function of \(\mathbf x\): evaluate at a point to get numbers.
  • Row \(i\): one output, every input.
  • Column \(j\): every output, one input.
Quick check (a separate example, not our data): for \(\mathbf f(\mathbf x)=\left(x_1^2x_2,\;3x_1+x_2^2\right)^\top\), what size is \(J_{\mathbf f}\)? Write \(J_{\mathbf f}\), then evaluate it at \(\mathbf x=(1,2)^\top\).
  • Size \(2\times2\): two outputs, two inputs.
  • \(J_{\mathbf f}=\begin{bmatrix}2x_1x_2&x_1^2\\3&2x_2\end{bmatrix}\)
  • At \((1,2)^\top\): \(\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\3&4\end{bmatrix}\)
"In the quick check, have them name which entry each partial fills before it appears."

The Jacobian of the prediction stage is \(X\)

The stage \(\hat{\mathbf y}=X\mathbf w\), one component per student:

  • \(\hat y_1=w_0,\quad \hat y_2=w_0+w_1,\quad \hat y_3=w_0+2w_1\)
  • \(2\) inputs, \(3\) outputs \(\;\Rightarrow\;\) \(J_{\hat{\mathbf y}}\) is \(3\times2\), one row per prediction:
\[J_{\hat{\mathbf y}}=\frac{d\hat{\mathbf y}}{d\mathbf w}=\begin{bmatrix} \partial\hat y_1/\partial w_0&\partial\hat y_1/\partial w_1\\ \partial\hat y_2/\partial w_0&\partial\hat y_2/\partial w_1\\ \partial\hat y_3/\partial w_0&\partial\hat y_3/\partial w_1\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}=X\]

The residual stage: Jacobian \(-I\)

Working with: \(\mathbf r=\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}\), \(\;\R^3\to\R^3\).

  • \(r_i=y_i-\hat y_i\) contains only its own \(\hat y_i\).
  • So \(\partial r_i/\partial\hat y_i=-1\), and every other entry is \(0\):
\[J_{\mathbf r}=\frac{d\mathbf r}{d\hat{\mathbf y}}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&-1\end{bmatrix}=-I\quad(3\times3)\]
  • The zeros of \(-I\): one student’s residual does not respond to another student’s prediction.
  • The diagonal \(-1\) is L13’s \(dr_i/d\hat y_i\).
"The minus sign in -2 r^T X lives on the diagonal of -I. The zeros are why the students separate cleanly."

The error stage: Jacobian \(2\mathbf r^\top\)

Working with: \(\mathrm{SSE}=\mathbf r^\top\mathbf r=r_1^2+r_2^2+r_3^2\), \(\;\R^3\to\R\).

  • \(\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}/\partial r_i=2r_i\) (MML (5.81), p.154):
\[J_{\mathrm{SSE}}=\frac{d\,\mathrm{SSE}}{d\mathbf r}=\begin{bmatrix}2r_1&2r_2&2r_3\end{bmatrix}=2\,\mathbf r^\top\quad(1\times3)\]
  • Each \(2r_i\) is L13’s \(de_i/dr_i\).
  • \(X\) and \(-I\) are the same at every point; \(2\mathbf r^\top\) changes with the point, through \(\mathbf r\).
The error stage has one output, so its Jacobian is one row. A row of partial derivatives of one output already has a name in this course.

A one-row Jacobian is the gradient

  • That is exactly L11’s gradient \(\nabla g\). [MASTER]
  • MML calls the row in (5.40) “the gradient of \(f\) or the Jacobian” (p.146; again after Def. 5.6, p.150).

The chain rule, matrix form

Chain rule, matrix form (MML (5.48), p.148). [MASTER] Let \(\mathbf f:\R^n\to\R^k\) be differentiable at \(\mathbf w\), and \(\mathbf g:\R^k\to\R^m\) differentiable at \(\mathbf f(\mathbf w)\). Then \[J_{\mathbf g\circ\mathbf f}(\mathbf w)=J_{\mathbf g}\big(\mathbf f(\mathbf w)\big)\;J_{\mathbf f}(\mathbf w),\qquad\text{sizes: }(m\times n)=(m\times k)(k\times n).\]
  • Stated, not proved here.
  • \(J_{\mathbf g}\) is evaluated at \(\mathbf f(\mathbf w)\), the point the inner stage delivers — not at \(\mathbf w\).
"Same rule as L13, with products of numbers replaced by products of matrices."

Reading the chain rule

Working with: \(J_{\mathbf g\circ\mathbf f}(\mathbf w)=J_{\mathbf g}\big(\mathbf f(\mathbf w)\big)\,J_{\mathbf f}(\mathbf w)\), middle variables \(v_1,\dots,v_k\).

  • Entry \((i,j)\) is row times column over the \(k\) middle variables: \[\frac{\partial(\mathbf g\circ\mathbf f)_i}{\partial w_j}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial v_l}\,\frac{\partial v_l}{\partial w_j}\]
  • If \(n=k=m=1\): every Jacobian is \(1\times1\), and the rule is L13’s \[\frac{dz}{dw}=\frac{dz}{dv}\cdot\frac{dv}{dw}\]

Three stages as one chain: \((2\mathbf r^\top)(-I)X=-2\mathbf r^\top X\) at every \(\mathbf w\)

Apply \(J_{\mathbf g\circ\mathbf f}=J_{\mathbf g}J_{\mathbf f}\) twice along:

\[\mathbf w\in\R^2\;\to\;\hat{\mathbf y}=X\mathbf w\in\R^3\;\to\;\mathbf r=\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}\in\R^3\;\to\;\mathrm{SSE}=\mathbf r^\top\mathbf r\in\R\]
\[\begin{aligned}\frac{d\,\mathrm{SSE}}{d\mathbf w} &=\underbrace{\frac{d\,\mathrm{SSE}}{d\mathbf r}}_{\substack{2\mathbf r^\top\\ 1\times3}}\quad\underbrace{\frac{d\mathbf r}{d\hat{\mathbf y}}}_{\substack{-I\\ 3\times3}}\quad\underbrace{\frac{d\hat{\mathbf y}}{d\mathbf w}}_{\substack{X\\ 3\times2}}\\[4pt] &=-2\,\mathbf r^\top X=\begin{bmatrix}-2(r_1+r_2+r_3)&-2(r_2+2r_3)\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf r=\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\end{aligned}\]
  • \(-I\) only flips every sign.
  • The result is \(1\times2\), a gradient’s shape, with no transpose needed (MML Remark, p.147).
"The phrase 'with r = y - Xw' is the 'evaluated at g(w)' part of the rule."
