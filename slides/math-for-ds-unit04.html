What this unit is for: a better model

  • A model is a rule that predicts. Unit 2 (L04) predicted a student’s quiz points \(y\) from the hours studied \(t\) with a line: \(\hat y=w_0+w_1t\).
  • The rule is fixed once we choose the two numbers \(w_0,w_1\). They are the model’s parameters, \(\mathbf w=(w_0,w_1)^\top\). Choosing the parameters is choosing the model.
  • How good is a model? L04’s mean squared error: \(\mathrm{MSE}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat y_i)^2\), the average squared residual. Better model = smaller error.
  • Three students: \(\mathbf t=(0,1,2)^\top\) hours, \(\mathbf y=(1,3,2)^\top\) points. With \(\mathbf w=(0,1)^\top\) the rule \(\hat y=t\) has \(\mathrm{MSE}=\frac53\); with \(\mathbf w=(1,1)^\top\) the rule \(\hat y=1+t\) has \(\mathrm{MSE}=\frac23\).
  • Changing \(w_0\) from \(0\) to \(1\) lowered the error from \(\frac53\) to \(\frac23\): a better model is better parameters. Changing the parameters so that the error goes down is training the model.
The question of this unit: if I change the parameters a little, how does the error change — does it go down, and which change lowers it fastest?
"Start from the model they know from L04, not from the word 'parameter'. A line is two numbers; pick the numbers, you have picked the line." "Ask: I just got from 5/3 to 2/3 by guessing. Can we do better than guessing? Which way should I change w0 and w1, and by how much? That is the whole unit."

L09 — From One Slope to Many

Reading: MML §5.2, pp.146–148 (Definition 5.5, partial derivatives, p.146). §5.1 pp.141–145 is recall.

You have had Calculus 1 and 2. The first slides are recall, stated in the language of error; the lecture itself is about a model with more than one parameter.

Recall, in the language of error

Three facts from Calculus 1, which is all we need from it. [MASTER]

  • \(f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\) is the change in error per unit change in the parameter (MML Def. 5.2, p.141).
  • First-order prediction: \(f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)\approx f'(x)\,\Delta x\) for small \(\Delta x\). The derivative does not just report a slope; it predicts what a small parameter change does to the error.
  • At an interior lowest point \(f'(x)=0\) — necessary, not sufficient. Two functions with \(f'(0)=0\) where \(0\) is not the lowest point: \(f(x)=-x^2\), a hill, highest at \(0\); and \(f(x)=x^3\), which keeps rising through \(0\).
"Three facts, nothing more from Calculus 1. The third one we will use as a tool later today: it is the whole proof of the theorem about partial derivatives." Point at the counterexamples: flat is necessary for lowest, not enough.

The first-order prediction with numbers: error and slope at \(x=17\)

Unit 2's first example: three friends guess your age, \(19\), \(21\), \(20\). Our single answer is \(x\); each guess minus \(x\) is one residual, and the error adds the three squared residuals:

\[f(x)=(19-x)^2+(21-x)^2+(20-x)^2 .\]
  • Error at \(x=17\). Residuals \(19-17=2\), \(21-17=4\), \(20-17=3\). Squares \(4,\ 16,\ 9\). Sum: \(f(17)=4+16+9=29.\)
  • Derivative. \(f'(x)=-2(19-x)-2(21-x)-2(20-x)=-120+6x\).
  • Slope at \(17\). \(f'(17)=-120+6\cdot17=-120+102=-18\).
  • Predict the error after a step \(\Delta x=0.1\), to \(x=17.1\): \(\;f(17.1)\approx f(17)+f'(17)\,\Delta x=29+(-18)(0.1)=29-1.8=\mathbf{27.2}\).
"Every number on this slide is one line of arithmetic. Do the residuals out loud: 2, 4, 3." "Students saw f'(x) = -120 + 6x in Unit 2; here it is only recalled." "Minus 18 means: near 17, each small step up in x lowers the error by about 18 per unit of step."

Check the prediction: the actual error at \(x=17.1\)

The error: \(f(x)=(19-x)^2+(21-x)^2+(20-x)^2\).
The prediction: \(f(17.1)\approx f(17)+f'(17)(0.1)=29+(-18)(0.1)=27.2\).

  • Actual value. Put \(x=17.1\) into \(f\): \[\begin{aligned}f(17.1)&=(19-17.1)^2+(21-17.1)^2+(20-17.1)^2\\&=1.9^2+3.9^2+2.9^2\\&=3.61+15.21+8.41=\mathbf{27.23}.\end{aligned}\]
  • The gap. \(27.23-27.2=0.03\). Close, not equal: the prediction follows the tangent line, and the curve bends away from it.

Score one rule: residuals and the sum of squared errors

Data: \(\mathbf t=(0,1,2)^\top\) hours, \(\mathbf y=(1,3,2)^\top\) points. Try the rule \(\hat y=t\).

  • Predictions: \(\hat y=0,\,1,\,2\).
  • Residuals \(r=y-\hat y\) (data minus prediction): \(1-0=1\), \(3-1=2\), \(2-2=0\).
  • Squared and added: \(1^2+2^2+0^2=5\) — three times L04’s \(\mathrm{MSE}=\tfrac53\).
Definition (MML §9.2.1, p.294, eq. (9.10b) without its constant factor). The sum of squared errors is \(\mathrm{SSE}=r_1^2+r_2^2+r_3^2=\norm{\mathbf r}^2\). “Best” = smallest \(\mathrm{SSE}\). [MASTER]
  • Rules to try: first \(\hat y=wt\) (\(w\) = points per hour; it predicts \(0\) at \(0\) hours). But student 1 scored \(1\) after \(0\) hours, so later \(\hat y=w_0+w_1t\): baseline \(w_0\), plus \(w_1\) points per hour.
"Let them compute the three residuals before revealing." "Why SSE and not L04's MSE comes up again once SSE(w) has a formula."

One parameter: the error \(\mathrm{SSE}(w)\), one student at a time

Rule \(\hat y=wt\). Each student gives one residual and one square:

  • Student 1 (\(t=0,\ y=1\)): prediction \(w\cdot0=0\), residual \(1-0=1\), square \(1\).
  • Student 2 (\(t=1,\ y=3\)): prediction \(w\), residual \(3-w\), square \((3-w)^2=9-6w+w^2\).
  • Student 3 (\(t=2,\ y=2\)): prediction \(2w\), residual \(2-2w\), square \((2-2w)^2=4-8w+4w^2\).
  • Collect like terms. \(w^2\): \(1+4=5\).  \(w\): \(-6-8=-14\).  Constants: \(1+9+4=14\).
\[\mathrm{SSE}(w)=1+(3-w)^2+(2-2w)^2=5w^2-14w+14 .\]

Check at \(w=1\): directly \(1+2^2+0^2=5\); from the formula \(5-14+14=5\) ✓. In the figure, each choice of \(w\) is one line \(\hat y=wt\) (top) and one point on the curve \(\mathrm{SSE}(w)=5w^2-14w+14\) (bottom); the lowest point, at \(w=\tfrac75\), is derived on the next slide.

"Three residuals, three squares, then collect. The constant 14 is 1² + 3² + 2²: the error of predicting 0 for everybody."

One parameter: the lowest point

Data \(\mathbf t=(0,1,2)^\top\) hours, \(\mathbf y=(1,3,2)^\top\) points; rule \(\hat y=wt\); error \(\mathrm{SSE}(w)=(1-0\cdot w)^2+(3-w)^2+(2-2w)^2=5w^2-14w+14\).

  • Derivative, term by term: \(\frac{d}{dw}5w^2=10w\), \(\frac{d}{dw}(-14w)=-14\), \(\frac{d}{dw}14=0\). So \(\mathrm{SSE}'(w)=10w-14\).
  • Flat point: \(10w-14=0\) gives \(w=\frac{14}{10}=\frac75\).
  • Lowest, not just flat: the \(w^2\) coefficient \(5\) is positive, so the parabola opens upward and its flat point is its lowest point. Only now does it earn the name \(w^\ast=\frac75\).
  • Error there: residuals \(1\), \(3-\frac75=\frac85\), \(2-\frac{14}5=-\frac45\); squares \(\frac{25}{25},\frac{64}{25},\frac{16}{25}\); \(\mathrm{SSE}(w^\ast)=\frac{105}{25}=\frac{21}{5}=4.2\).
Is \(w^\ast=\frac75\) related to anything we have seen before — or just a number that came out of this data?
Point at the figure: the best line through the origin still misses student 1 by exactly 1 — no w can fix that, since every w predicts 0 at 0 hours.

Why \(w^\ast\) is L03’s projection fraction — for any data, not by luck

Any data: hours \(\mathbf t=(t_1,\dots,t_n)^\top\), points \(\mathbf y=(y_1,\dots,y_n)^\top\); rule \(\hat y=wt\); error \(\mathrm{SSE}(w)=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-wt_i)^2\).

  • Expand one term: \((y_i-wt_i)^2=y_i^2-2w\,t_iy_i+w^2t_i^2\).
  • Add over the \(n\) students: \(\mathrm{SSE}(w)=\big(\sum t_i^2\big)w^2-2\big(\sum t_iy_i\big)w+\sum y_i^2=(\mathbf t^\top\mathbf t)\,w^2-2\,(\mathbf t^\top\mathbf y)\,w+\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y\).
  • Our data: \(\mathbf t^\top\mathbf t=0^2+1^2+2^2=5\), \(\mathbf t^\top\mathbf y=0\cdot1+1\cdot3+2\cdot2=7\), \(\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y=1+9+4=14\): that is why \(\mathrm{SSE}(w)=5w^2-14w+14\). The three coefficients are these dot products.
  • Derivative: \(\mathrm{SSE}'(w)=2(\mathbf t^\top\mathbf t)\,w-2\,\mathbf t^\top\mathbf y=-2\,\mathbf t^\top(\mathbf y-w\mathbf t)=-2\,\mathbf t^\top\mathbf r\), with residual \(\mathbf r=\mathbf y-w\mathbf t\).
  • Set it to zero (with \(\mathbf t\neq\mathbf 0\)): \(w^\ast=\dfrac{\mathbf t^\top\mathbf y}{\mathbf t^\top\mathbf t}\), for every dataset. Ours: \(\frac75\).

This is L03’s projection formula \(\lambda=\dfrac{\mathbf b^\top\mathbf x}{\mathbf b^\top\mathbf b}\), with \(\mathbf b=\mathbf t\) (the direction) and \(\mathbf x=\mathbf y\) (the vector projected). And \(\mathbf t^\top\mathbf r=0\) is L03’s picture: at the best \(w\), the residual is perpendicular to \(\mathbf t\).

"Calculus (set the derivative to zero) lands on the geometry of L03 (projection onto t) — as an identity, for any data. Do not let them think 7/5 = 7/5 is the argument: the argument is that the coefficients of SSE are the dot products."

Why SSE, and not L04's MSE?

L04 measured a fit by the mean squared error \(\mathrm{MSE}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat y_i)^2\). Here \(n=3\) students, so \(\mathrm{MSE}=\mathrm{SSE}/3\). Our one-parameter error: \(\mathrm{SSE}(w)=(1-0\cdot w)^2+(3-w)^2+(2-2w)^2=5w^2-14w+14\).

  • Same best parameter. Dividing by the positive number \(3\) keeps every comparison: \(\mathrm{SSE}(a)\le\mathrm{SSE}(b)\) exactly when \(\mathrm{SSE}(a)/3\le\mathrm{SSE}(b)/3\).
  • Same flat point. \(\mathrm{MSE}(w)=\frac53w^2-\frac{14}3w+\frac{14}3\), so \(\mathrm{MSE}'(w)=\frac{10}3w-\frac{14}3=\frac{10w-14}{3}\): the \(\mathrm{SSE}\) derivative \(\mathrm{SSE}'(w)=10w-14\) times \(\frac13\), zero at the same \(w=\frac75\).
  • Different value. At \(w^\ast=\frac75\): \(\mathrm{SSE}=\frac{21}5\) and \(\mathrm{MSE}=\frac{21}5\div3=\frac{7}{5}\). (That this error value equals \(w^\ast\) is a coincidence.)
  • Why this unit uses SSE: fewer fractions. \(5w^2-14w+14\) has whole-number coefficients; the MSE version has thirds everywhere. With two parameters the difference grows.
Every “best” answer in this unit is the same for SSE and MSE. Only the size of the error, and the arithmetic, change.

The error for \(\hat y=w_0+w_1t\): \(\mathrm{SSE}(w_0,w_1)=(1-w_0)^2+(3-w_0-w_1)^2+(2-w_0-2w_1)^2\). Each square has the shape \((a-b-c)^2\), and

\[(a-b-c)^2=a^2+b^2+c^2-2ab-2ac+2bc .\]

  • \((3-w_0-w_1)^2\) (\(a=3,\ b=w_0,\ c=w_1\)):

  • \((2-w_0-2w_1)^2\) (\(a=2,\ b=w_0,\ c=2w_1\)):

Freeze one parameter: a slice of the error surface, and its slope

The error we are working with: \(\mathrm{SSE}(w_0,w_1)=3w_0^2+6w_0w_1\) \(+5w_1^2-12w_0-14w_1+14\).

  • Hold \(w_1\) fixed at \(0\). The points with \(w_1=0\) form a vertical plane (pink). The plane cuts the error surface along a curve (red).
  • On the red curve only \(w_0\) moves. Put \(w_1=0\) into the error; every term with \(w_1\) vanishes: \(\mathrm{SSE}(w_0,0)=3w_0^2-12w_0+14\).
  • Its slope is the ordinary Calculus 1 derivative \(\frac{d}{dw_0}\big(3w_0^2-12w_0+14\big)=6w_0-12\).
  • At \(w_0=0\): height \(\mathrm{SSE}(0,0)=14\) (the dot), slope \(6\cdot0-12=-12\) (the dashed tangent).
  • Hold \(w_0\) fixed at \(0\) instead: the plane \(w_0=0\) cuts the curve \(\mathrm{SSE}(0,w_1)=5w_1^2-14w_1+14\), with slope \(10w_1-14\), which is \(-14\) at \(w_1=0\).
Two slopes at \((0,0)^\top\): \(-12\) along \(w_0\), \(-14\) along \(w_1\). Each is an ordinary derivative taken with the other parameter frozen — a kind of derivative that gets its own name.
"This is the picture behind the definition: freeze w1, and the surface becomes an ordinary curve; its Calculus 1 slope is the partial derivative." Change one parameter, hold the other still

The error we are working with: \(\mathrm{SSE}(w_0,w_1)=(1-w_0)^2+(3-w_0-w_1)^2+(2-w_0-2w_1)^2\) \(=3w_0^2+6w_0w_1+5w_1^2-12w_0-14w_1+14\).

Definition (MML Def. 5.5, p.146). The partial derivative of \(g(w_0,w_1)\) with respect to \(w_0\) is \[\frac{\partial g}{\partial w_0}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{g(w_0+h,\;w_1)-g(w_0,\;w_1)}{h},\] the ordinary derivative of \(w_0\mapsto g(w_0,w_1)\) with \(w_1\) held fixed; similarly for \(w_1\). [MASTER]
  • The two slices at \((0,0)^\top\): \(\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}}{\partial w_0}(0,0)=-12\) (freeze \(w_1=0\)), \(\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}}{\partial w_1}(0,0)=-14\) (freeze \(w_0=0\)): slopes of the error surface along the axes. [MASTER]
  • Nothing new to compute with: treat the other parameter as a constant and use the rules from Calculus 1.
  • Example: the term \(6w_0w_1\) with respect to \(w_0\). With \(w_1\) held fixed, \(6w_0w_1=(6w_1)\cdot w_0\) is a constant times \(w_0\). Its derivative is \(6w_1\).
  • The same term with respect to \(w_1\). Now \(w_0\) is held fixed: \(6w_0w_1=(6w_0)\cdot w_1\), derivative \(6w_0\).
  • A term without \(w_0\), like \(5w_1^2\), is a constant when \(w_1\) is held fixed: its \(w_0\) partial derivative is \(0\).
"The definition is the Calculus 1 limit with one coordinate moving and the other nailed down. That is all a partial derivative is." "The mixed term is the only place students slip: they differentiate both factors. Hold one still."

Why every partial derivative of SSE is zero at a lowest point

Theorem. Let \(\mathrm{SSE}\) be differentiable. If it is smallest at an interior point \((w_0^\ast,w_1^\ast)^\top\) — no boundary, and here the parameters range over all of \(\R^2\) — then both partial derivatives are zero there. [MASTER]

Our error: \(\mathrm{SSE}(w_0,w_1)=(1-w_0)^2+(3-w_0-w_1)^2+(2-w_0-2w_1)^2\) \(=3w_0^2+6w_0w_1+5w_1^2-12w_0-14w_1+14\).  Proof.

  • Step 1: freeze \(w_1=w_1^\ast\). Define the one-variable function \(u(w_0)=\mathrm{SSE}(w_0,w_1^\ast)\). For our SSE: \(u(w_0)=3w_0^2+(6w_1^\ast-12)\,w_0+(5w_1^{\ast2}-14w_1^\ast+14)\).
  • Step 2: \(u\) is smallest at \(w_0^\ast\). \(\mathrm{SSE}(w_0^\ast,w_1^\ast)\le\mathrm{SSE}(w_0,w_1)\) for every pair \((w_0,w_1)^\top\), so also for the pairs \((w_0,w_1^\ast)^\top\). That says \(u(w_0^\ast)\le u(w_0)\) for every \(w_0\).
  • Step 3: use the Calculus 1 fact. \(w_0\) ranges over all of \(\R\), so \(w_0^\ast\) is interior. Calculus 1: at an interior lowest point of a differentiable one-variable function the derivative is \(0\). So \(u'(w_0^\ast)=0\).
  • Step 4: \(u'\) is the partial derivative. \(u'\) differentiates in \(w_0\) with \(w_1\) held at \(w_1^\ast\) — Def. 5.5. So \(\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}}{\partial w_0}(w_0^\ast,w_1^\ast)=0\). Ours: \(u'(w_0)=6w_0+6w_1^\ast-12\), which is \(\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}}{\partial w_0}=6w_0+6w_1-12\) at \(w_1=w_1^\ast\).
  • Step 5: the other one. Freeze \(w_0=w_0^\ast\) instead: \(v(w_1)=\mathrm{SSE}(w_0^\ast,w_1)\), and Steps 2–4 give \(\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}}{\partial w_1}(w_0^\ast,w_1^\ast)=0\). Ours: \(v'(w_1)=6w_0^\ast+10w_1-14\), which is \(\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}}{\partial w_1}=6w_0+10w_1-14\) at \(w_0=w_0^\ast\).
"Each step is one line. The whole proof is: restrict to a line, and a lowest point on the surface is still a lowest point on the line." "Two equations, two unknowns — and each one came from a single-variable fact they already had." "Do not call anything w* yet on the board after this slide: the theorem goes from 'lowest' to 'flat', not back."

Theorem: the flat point is a global minimum — and the plan of its proof

\[\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\norm{\mathbf y-X\mathbf w}^2,\quad \mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}1\\3\\2\end{bmatrix},\quad X=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf w_{\text{flat}}=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac32\\[2pt]\tfrac12\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=\mathbf y-X\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}=\begin{bmatrix}-\tfrac12\\1\\-\tfrac12\end{bmatrix}\]
Theorem (Pythagoras for least squares). For every \(\mathbf w\in\R^2\), \[\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w_{\text{flat}})+\norm{X(\mathbf w-\mathbf w_{\text{flat}})}^2 .\] In particular \(\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)\ge\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w_{\text{flat}})=\norm{\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}}^2=\tfrac14+1+\tfrac14=\tfrac32\): the flat point is a global minimum. [MASTER]

The proof has four steps, one per slide, each checked at \(\mathbf w=(2,-1)^\top\):

  1. Split. Write \(\mathbf w=\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}+\mathbf d\); then the residual of \(\mathbf w\) is \(\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}-X\mathbf d\).
  2. Read the flat condition geometrically. \(\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}/\partial w_0=\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}/\partial w_1=0\) \(\iff X^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=\mathbf 0\).
  3. Orthogonality. From \(X^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=\mathbf 0\): \(\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}\) is orthogonal to \(X\mathbf d\), whatever \(\mathbf d\) is.
  4. Pythagoras. Expand \(\norm{\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}-X\mathbf d}^2\); the cross term is zero.

Step 1: split any \(\mathbf w\) into the flat point plus a step

Notation. The residual of \(\mathbf w\) is \(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\), and \(\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\norm{\mathbf y-X\mathbf w}^2\). The flat point is \(\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}=\left(\tfrac32,\tfrac12\right)^\top\), its residual \(\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=\mathbf y-X\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}\).

Take any \(\mathbf w\). Call the step from the flat point to it \(\;\mathbf d=\mathbf w-\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}\), so \(\;\mathbf w=\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}+\mathbf d\).

  • Residual of \(\mathbf w\): \(\;\mathbf y-X\mathbf w=\mathbf y-X(\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}+\mathbf d)\).
  • A matrix times a sum is the sum of the products: \(\;X(\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}+\mathbf d)=X\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}+X\mathbf d\).
  • So \(\;\mathbf y-X\mathbf w=\mathbf y-X\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}-X\mathbf d\).
  • The first two terms, \(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}\), are \(\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}\). Therefore \[\mathbf y-X\mathbf w=\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}-X\mathbf d .\]
  • In words: the residual at any \(\mathbf w\) is the flat point’s residual, minus the change in predictions \(X\mathbf d\) caused by the step \(\mathbf d\).

So \(\;\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\norm{\mathbf y-X\mathbf w}^2=\norm{\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}-X\mathbf d}^2\). The rest of the proof is about the squared length \(\norm{\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}-X\mathbf d}^2\). [MASTER]

Step 2: \(\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}/\partial w_0=\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}/\partial w_1=0\) says \(X^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=\mathbf 0\)

  • The two partial derivatives of \(\mathrm{SSE}\) set to zero: \(\;6w_0+6w_1-12=0\), \(\;6w_0+10w_1-14=0\). Divide by 2: \(\;3w_0+3w_1=6\), \(\;3w_0+5w_1=7\).
  • The halved pair is \(X^\top X\mathbf w=X^\top\mathbf y\), with solution \(\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}=\left(\tfrac32,\tfrac12\right)^\top\): entries of \(X^\top X\) are \(1+1+1=3\), \(0+1+2=3\), \(0+1+4=5\), and \(X^\top\mathbf y=(1+3+2,\;0+3+4)^\top=(6,7)^\top\).
  • Move everything to one side: \(\;X^\top\mathbf y-X^\top X\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}=\mathbf 0\).
  • Factor \(X^\top\) out on the left: \(\;X^\top(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w_{\text{flat}})=\mathbf 0\).
  • The bracket \(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}\) is the flat point’s residual \(\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}\): \(\;X^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=\mathbf 0\).
  • Each move can be undone: \(\;X^\top X\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}=X^\top\mathbf y\iff X^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=\mathbf 0\).

Calculus (both slopes of \(\mathrm{SSE}\) zero) and geometry (L04: residual \(\perp\) every column of \(X\)) give the same equation. [MASTER]

\(X^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=\mathbf 0\) entry by entry: \(\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}\) is orthogonal to each column of \(X\)

Notation. \(\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=\mathbf y-X\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}=\left(1-\tfrac32,\;3-2,\;2-\tfrac52\right)^\top=\left(-\tfrac12,1,-\tfrac12\right)^\top\), from \(\mathbf y=(1,3,2)^\top\), \(\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}=\left(\tfrac32,\tfrac12\right)^\top\). The columns of \(X\) are the ones column \(\mathbf 1=(1,1,1)^\top\) and the hours column \(\mathbf t=(0,1,2)^\top\); they are the rows of \(X^\top\).

\[X^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\0&1&2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-\tfrac12\\1\\-\tfrac12\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}1\left(-\tfrac12\right)+1(1)+1\left(-\tfrac12\right)\\[2pt]0\left(-\tfrac12\right)+1(1)+2\left(-\tfrac12\right)\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}-\tfrac12+1-\tfrac12\\[2pt]0+1-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}\]
  • Entry 1 is \(\mathbf 1^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=0\): the three residuals add up to zero.
  • Entry 2 is \(\mathbf t^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=0\): each residual times that student’s hours, added up, is zero.
  • An inner product of zero is exactly what orthogonal means (MML Def. 3.7, p.77). So \(\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}\perp\mathbf 1\) and \(\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}\perp\mathbf t\).

One sentence to remember: at the flat point, the residual is orthogonal to every column of \(X\). [MASTER]

Step 3: \(\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}\) is orthogonal to \(X\mathbf d\), for every step \(\mathbf d\)

Notation. Columns of \(X\): \(\mathbf 1=(1,1,1)^\top\), \(\mathbf t=(0,1,2)^\top\). \(\;\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=\left(-\tfrac12,1,-\tfrac12\right)^\top\), and \(X^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=\mathbf 0\) (Step 2), i.e. \(\mathbf 1^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=0\) and \(\mathbf t^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=0\).

\(X\mathbf d=d_0\,\mathbf 1+d_1\,\mathbf t\) is a combination of the columns of \(X\). The inner product \((X\mathbf d)^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}\), in two ways:

  • Column by column: \(\;(X\mathbf d)^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=d_0\,\underbrace{\mathbf 1^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}}_{0}+d_1\,\underbrace{\mathbf t^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}}_{0}=0\).
  • In one line, using \((AB)^\top=B^\top A^\top\): \(\;(X\mathbf d)^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=\mathbf d^\top X^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=\mathbf d^\top\big(X^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}\big)=\mathbf d^\top\mathbf 0=0\).
  • No condition on \(\mathbf d\) was used: whatever step we take, the change in predictions \(X\mathbf d\) is orthogonal to \(\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}\).

Check at \(\mathbf w=(2,-1)^\top\): \(\mathbf d=\left(2-\tfrac32,\,-1-\tfrac12\right)^\top=\left(\tfrac12,-\tfrac32\right)^\top\), \(X\mathbf d=\left(\tfrac12,\;\tfrac12-\tfrac32,\;\tfrac12-3\right)^\top=\left(\tfrac12,-1,-\tfrac52\right)^\top\). \[(X\mathbf d)^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=\tfrac12\left(-\tfrac12\right)+(-1)(1)+\left(-\tfrac52\right)\left(-\tfrac12\right)=-\tfrac14-1+\tfrac54=0\;✓\]

Step 4: Pythagoras — the cross term vanishes

Notation. \(\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\norm{\mathbf y-X\mathbf w}^2\), \(\mathbf d=\mathbf w-\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}\). Step 1: \(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w=\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}-X\mathbf d\). Step 3: \((X\mathbf d)^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=0\).

For any two vectors \(\mathbf a,\mathbf b\), multiply out \(\norm{\mathbf a-\mathbf b}^2=(\mathbf a-\mathbf b)^\top(\mathbf a-\mathbf b)\) term by term (\(\mathbf b^\top\mathbf a\) and \(\mathbf a^\top\mathbf b\) are the same number):

\[\norm{\mathbf a-\mathbf b}^2=\mathbf a^\top\mathbf a-\mathbf a^\top\mathbf b-\mathbf b^\top\mathbf a+\mathbf b^\top\mathbf b=\norm{\mathbf a}^2-2\,\mathbf a^\top\mathbf b+\norm{\mathbf b}^2 .\]
  • Take \(\mathbf a=\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}\), \(\mathbf b=X\mathbf d\). By Step 1, \(\;\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\norm{\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}-X\mathbf d}^2=\norm{\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}}^2-2\,\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}^\top X\mathbf d+\norm{X\mathbf d}^2\).
  • The cross term \(\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}^\top X\mathbf d\) is the same number as \((X\mathbf d)^\top\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}=0\) (Step 3). What is left: \[\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\norm{\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}}^2+\norm{X\mathbf d}^2\;\ge\;\norm{\mathbf r_{\text{flat}}}^2=\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}) ,\] since \(\norm{X\mathbf d}^2\), a squared length, is never negative. So \(\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}\) is a global minimum.
  • Same shape as L03’s proof that the projection \(\lambda\mathbf b\) is the closest point to \(\mathbf x\) on the line through \(\mathbf b\): for any other point \(\mu\mathbf b\), \(\norm{\mathbf x-\mu\mathbf b}^2=\norm{\mathbf r}^2+(\lambda-\mu)^2\norm{\mathbf b}^2\), a fixed part plus a part that is never negative.

Now we have earned the names \(\mathbf w^\ast=\left(\tfrac32,\tfrac12\right)^\top\) and \(\mathbf r^\ast=\mathbf y-X\mathbf w^\ast=\left(-\tfrac12,1,-\tfrac12\right)^\top\). [MASTER]

What “global” adds over “flat”

Notation. \(\mathrm{SSE}(w_0,w_1)=3w_0^2+6w_0w_1+5w_1^2-12w_0-14w_1+14\), lowest at \(\mathbf w^\ast=\left(\tfrac32,\tfrac12\right)^\top\) with \(\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w^\ast)=\tfrac32\). Identity: \(\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\tfrac32+\norm{X(\mathbf w-\mathbf w^\ast)}^2\).

  • Flat (L09): at \(\mathbf w_{\text{flat}}\), the slopes along the two axes are zero. That says nothing about points farther away.
  • Global minimum (today): \(\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)\ge\tfrac32\) at every \(\mathbf w\). The height above the floor \(\tfrac32\) is \(\norm{X(\mathbf w-\mathbf w^\ast)}^2\), a squared length, never negative:

Is \(\mathbf w^\ast\) the only global minimum?

Notation. \(\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\norm{\mathbf y-X\mathbf w}^2\); \(X=[\,\mathbf 1\;\;\mathbf t\,]\) with columns \(\mathbf 1=(1,1,1)^\top\), \(\mathbf t=(0,1,2)^\top\); \(\mathbf w^\ast=\left(\tfrac32,\tfrac12\right)^\top\); Pythagoras identity \(\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w^\ast)+\norm{X(\mathbf w-\mathbf w^\ast)}^2\).

Uniqueness. \(\mathbf w^\ast\) is the only \(\mathbf w\) with \(\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w^\ast)\) exactly when the columns of \(X\) are linearly independent (MML Def. 2.12, p.40), that is, when \(\operatorname{rank}X\) equals the number of columns (MML §2.6.2, p.47). [MASTER]
  • From \(\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w^\ast)+\norm{X\mathbf d}^2\), with \(\mathbf d=\mathbf w-\mathbf w^\ast\): \(\;\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w^\ast)\iff\norm{X\mathbf d}^2=0\iff X\mathbf d=\mathbf 0\).
  • \(X\mathbf d=d_0\,\mathbf 1+d_1\,\mathbf t\). Independent columns means: \(d_0\,\mathbf 1+d_1\,\mathbf t=\mathbf 0\) only when \(d_0=d_1=0\). So \(\mathbf d=\mathbf 0\), i.e. \(\mathbf w=\mathbf w^\ast\).
  • Our \(X\): \(\;X\mathbf d=(d_0,\;d_0+d_1,\;d_0+2d_1)^\top=\mathbf 0\). The first entry gives \(d_0=0\); then the second gives \(d_1=0\). So \(\operatorname{rank}X=2=\) number of columns: \(\mathbf w^\ast\) is the only best answer.
  • Dependent columns means: some \(\mathbf d\neq\mathbf 0\) has \(X\mathbf d=\mathbf 0\). Then \(\mathbf w^\ast+\mathbf d\) has predictions \(X\mathbf w^\ast+X\mathbf d=X\mathbf w^\ast\): same predictions, same error, different \(\mathbf w\).
"Steps 1, 3 and 4 of the proof never used the entries of X, only X-transpose r = 0. So the identity, and this uniqueness argument, hold for any data matrix. The next slide uses that."

Pick a direction, and the surface becomes a curve

  • A direction is a column \(\mathbf u=(u_0,u_1)^\top\) with \(\norm{\mathbf u}=1\). Length \(1\) so that \(h\) is the distance walked: \(\norm{h\mathbf u}=|h|\,\norm{\mathbf u}=|h|\).
  • The straight line through \(\mathbf w\) in the direction \(\mathbf u\) is the set of points \(\mathbf w+h\mathbf u\), one point for each number \(h\).
  • Along that line the error depends on the single number \(h\): \(\;\phi(h)=\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w+h\mathbf u)\), and \(\phi(0)=\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)\) is where we stand.
  • Our example: \(\mathbf w=(0,0)^\top\) and \(\mathbf u=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)^\top\), on the error \(\mathrm{SSE}(w_0,w_1)=3w_0^2+6w_0w_1+5w_1^2-12w_0-14w_1+14\). Length check: \(\norm{\mathbf u}^2=\big(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big)^2+\big(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big)^2=\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1\) ✓.

One row answers every direction: name it the gradient

Definition (MML eq. (5.40), p.146). For \(g(\mathbf w)\) with \(\mathbf w\in\R^d\), the gradient is the row vector of partial derivatives \[\nabla g=\frac{d\,g}{d\mathbf w}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial w_0}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial w_{d-1}}\end{bmatrix}\in\R^{1\times d}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad D_{\mathbf u}g=\nabla g\,\mathbf u .\] [MASTER]
  • Every directional slope at \(\mathbf w\) is the same row \(\nabla g\) times a different column \(\mathbf u\). Shapes: \((1\times d)(d\times1)=1\times1\), one number.
  • Why a row? For a function with one output the derivative is naturally one row: in L14 the gradient turns out to be the Jacobian of a one-output function, and the chain rule becomes a plain matrix product (MML eq. (5.40), p.146; Remark “Gradient as a Row Vector”, p.147).
  • ⚠️ Mind the transpose. The gradient is a row; a direction to walk in is a column. The row \(\nabla g\) and the column \((\nabla g)^\top\) differ by a transpose, and this course writes the transpose out every time.
  • Our error, \(d=2\): \(\mathrm{SSE}(w_0,w_1)=3w_0^2+6w_0w_1+5w_1^2-12w_0-14w_1+14\), with \(\partial\mathrm{SSE}/\partial w_0=6w_0+6w_1-12\) and \(\partial\mathrm{SSE}/\partial w_1=6w_0+10w_1-14\), so \(\;\nabla\mathrm{SSE}=\begin{bmatrix}6w_0+6w_1-12&6w_0+10w_1-14\end{bmatrix}\).

Which direction is steepest? One step at a time

Assume \(\nabla g\neq\mathbf 0\) at \(\mathbf w\), and let \(\theta\) be the angle between the column \((\nabla g)^\top\) and the unit direction \(\mathbf u\).

  • Step 1. \(D_{\mathbf u}g=\nabla g\,\mathbf u=\big((\nabla g)^\top\big)^\top\mathbf u\): the dot product of the two columns \((\nabla g)^\top\) and \(\mathbf u\).
  • Step 2. L01’s cosine formula, \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{\mathbf x^\top\mathbf y}{\norm{\mathbf x}\,\norm{\mathbf y}}\), multiplied out: \(\;\mathbf x^\top\mathbf y=\norm{\mathbf x}\,\norm{\mathbf y}\cos\theta\). The cosine formula needs both vectors nonzero: \(\mathbf u\) has length \(1\), and that is why we assumed \(\nabla g\neq\mathbf 0\).
  • Step 3. Take \(\mathbf x=(\nabla g)^\top\), \(\mathbf y=\mathbf u\), and use \(\norm{\mathbf u}=1\): \[D_{\mathbf u}g=\norm{(\nabla g)^\top}\cos\theta .\]
  • Step 4. \(-1\le\cos\theta\le1\), so \(\;-\norm{(\nabla g)^\top}\le D_{\mathbf u}g\le\norm{(\nabla g)^\top}\).
  • Our numbers at \((0,0)^\top\): \(\nabla\mathrm{SSE}=\begin{bmatrix}6w_0+6w_1-12&6w_0+10w_1-14\end{bmatrix}\) gives \(\begin{bmatrix}-12&-14\end{bmatrix}\), so \(\norm{(\nabla\mathrm{SSE})^\top}=\sqrt{(-12)^2+(-14)^2}=\sqrt{144+196}=\sqrt{340}\approx18.439\). So every directional slope at \((0,0)^\top\) lies between \(-18.439\) and \(18.439\).
  • The slope along \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)^\top\), \(-13\sqrt2\approx-18.385\), fits: \(\cos\theta=\tfrac{-18.385}{18.439}\approx-0.997\), almost the lowest possible value.

Three angles, three answers

Write the error as a product of two matrices

General sizes: \(n\) data points, \(d\) parameters. \(X\) is \(n\times d\), \(\mathbf w\) is \(d\times1\), \(\mathbf y\) is \(n\times1\). Ours: \(n=3\), \(d=2\).

  • The residual \(\mathbf r=\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\) is \(n\times1\), and a squared length is a vector dotted with itself: \(\;\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\norm{\mathbf y-X\mathbf w}^2=(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)^\top(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)\).
  • Transpose the first factor, \(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\). A transpose of a product reverses the order, \((X\mathbf w)^\top=\mathbf w^\top X^\top\), so \(\;(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)^\top=\mathbf y^\top-\mathbf w^\top X^\top\).
  • Multiply \((\mathbf y^\top-\mathbf w^\top X^\top)(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)\) out like \((a-b)(c-e)=ac-ae-bc+be\), keeping every product in its order (matrices do not commute):
Left piece \(\times\) right pieceProductSizes multipliedResult
\(\mathbf y^\top\times\mathbf y\)\(+\,\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y\)\((1\times n)(n\times1)\)\(1\times1\)
\(\mathbf y^\top\times(-X\mathbf w)\)\(-\,\mathbf y^\top X\mathbf w\)\((1\times n)(n\times d)(d\times1)\)\(1\times1\)
\((-\mathbf w^\top X^\top)\times\mathbf y\)\(-\,\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y\)\((1\times d)(d\times n)(n\times1)\)\(1\times1\)
\((-\mathbf w^\top X^\top)\times(-X\mathbf w)\)\(+\,\mathbf w^\top X^\top X\mathbf w\)\((1\times d)(d\times n)(n\times d)(d\times1)\)\(1\times1\)

So \(\;\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y-\mathbf y^\top X\mathbf w-\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y+\mathbf w^\top X^\top X\mathbf w\).

\(\mathbf y^\top X\mathbf w\) and \(\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y\) are the same number

Multiplying out \((\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)^\top(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)\) gave four products: \(\;\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y-\mathbf y^\top X\mathbf w-\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y+\mathbf w^\top X^\top X\mathbf w\).

  • The two middle products, \(\mathbf y^\top X\mathbf w\) and \(\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y\), are \(1\times1\): each is a single number.
  • A transpose of a product reverses the order, \((ABC)^\top=C^\top B^\top A^\top\), so \(\;(\mathbf y^\top X\mathbf w)^\top=\mathbf w^\top X^\top(\mathbf y^\top)^\top=\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y\).
  • A \(1\times1\) matrix equals its own transpose, \([c]^\top=[c]\), so \(\;\mathbf y^\top X\mathbf w=(\mathbf y^\top X\mathbf w)^\top=\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y\).
  • On our data (\(\mathbf y=(1,3,2)^\top\), \(X\) with rows \((1,0),(1,1),(1,2)\)): \(\mathbf y^\top X=\begin{bmatrix}1+3+2&0+3+4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}6&7\end{bmatrix}\) and \(X^\top\mathbf y=(6,7)^\top\), so both products are \(6w_0+7w_1\) ✓.
  • So the middle terms of \(\mathrm{SSE}\) combine: \(\;-\mathbf y^\top X\mathbf w-\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y=-2\,\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y\).
\[\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\underbrace{\mathbf w^\top X^\top X\mathbf w}_{\text{quadratic in }\mathbf w}\;-\;\underbrace{2\,\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y}_{\text{linear in }\mathbf w}\;+\;\underbrace{\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y}_{\text{no }\mathbf w}\] [MASTER]

The three matrix pieces of SSE, on our data

The pieces of \(\;\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\mathbf w^\top X^\top X\mathbf w-2\,\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y+\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y\), with \(\mathbf y=(1,3,2)^\top\) and \(X\) with columns \(\mathbf 1=(1,1,1)^\top\) (intercept) and \(\mathbf t=(0,1,2)^\top\) (hours). Check: the matrix form of SSE expands to the polynomial

Matrix form \(\;\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\mathbf w^\top X^\top X\mathbf w-2\,\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y+\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y\), with \(X^\top X=\begin{bmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix}\), \(X^\top\mathbf y=(6,7)^\top\), \(\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y=14\).

  • Quadratic piece, step 1. Matrix times column: \(\;X^\top X\,\mathbf w=\begin{bmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}w_0\\w_1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3w_0+3w_1\\3w_0+5w_1\end{bmatrix}\).
  • Quadratic piece, step 2. Row \(\mathbf w^\top\) times the column \(X^\top X\mathbf w\) from step 1: \[\begin{aligned}\mathbf w^\top X^\top X\mathbf w&=w_0(3w_0+3w_1)+w_1(3w_0+5w_1)\\&=3w_0^2+3w_0w_1+3w_0w_1+5w_1^2=3w_0^2+6w_0w_1+5w_1^2 .\end{aligned}\]
  • Linear piece. \(\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}w_0&w_1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}6\\7\end{bmatrix}=6w_0+7w_1\), so \(\;-2\,\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y=-12w_0-14w_1\).
  • Constant piece. \(\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y=14\).
  • Add the three pieces: \(\;3w_0^2+6w_0w_1+5w_1^2-12w_0-14w_1+14\) — exactly the polynomial L09 got by expanding \((1-w_0)^2+(3-w_0-w_1)^2+(2-w_0-2w_1)^2\) term by term ✓.
  • Notice where \(6w_0w_1\) came from: \(3w_0w_1+3w_0w_1\), one from each off-diagonal \(3\). The two \(3w_0w_1\) terms are equal because \(X^\top X\) is symmetric; Rule 2 will need that symmetry.

Two shapes to differentiate

Overall goal: prove \(\nabla\mathrm{SSE}=-2\,\mathbf r^\top X\) for any number of features.

What “differentiate with respect to the vector \(\mathbf w\)” means (L11, MML (5.40), p.146): \(\;\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf w}=\nabla f=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial w_0}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial w_{d-1}}\end{bmatrix}\), the row of partial derivatives (MML also writes \(df/d\mathbf w\)). So every rule below is proved component by component: one partial derivative at a time, the other parameters held fixed.

In \(\;\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\mathbf w^\top X^\top X\mathbf w-2\,\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y+\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y\;\) the middle term is a number, so it equals its transpose: \(\mathbf w^\top X^\top\mathbf y=(X^\top\mathbf y)^\top\mathbf w\). With \(\mathbf b=X^\top\mathbf y\), \(A=X^\top X\): \(\;\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w-2\,\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w+\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y\) — two shapes to differentiate, \(\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w\) and \(\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w\).

  • \(\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w\) is linear in \(\mathbf w\): every term has one parameter. Rule 1 differentiates it.
  • \(\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w\) is quadratic: every term has two parameters (or one, squared). Rule 2 differentiates it.
  • \(\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y\) has no \(\mathbf w\) in it, so its derivative is \(0\).

Rule 1: the derivative of a linear function

Overall goal: prove \(\nabla\mathrm{SSE}=-2\,\mathbf r^\top X\) for any number of features.

Look first: our \(\mathbf b=(6,7)^\top\): \(\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w=6w_0+7w_1\) has partial derivatives \(6\), \(7\): the row \(\begin{bmatrix}6&7\end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf b\) laid flat.

Rule 1 (MML (5.105), p.158). For a fixed column \(\mathbf b\in\R^d\): \(\;\dfrac{\partial(\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w)}{\partial\mathbf w}=\mathbf b^\top\). [MASTER]
  1. Proof. Write out \(\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w\): \(\;\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w=b_0w_0+b_1w_1+\cdots+b_{d-1}w_{d-1}\).
  2. Differentiate in \(w_j\), all other parameters held fixed: the term \(b_jw_j\) gives \(b_j\); every term \(b_kw_k\) with \(k\neq j\) is a constant and gives \(0\). So \(\partial(\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w)/\partial w_j=b_j\).
  3. The gradient is the row of partial derivatives (MML (5.40)): \(\;\begin{bmatrix}b_0&b_1&\cdots&b_{d-1}\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf b^\top\).

On our \(\mathbf b=(6,7)^\top\): the piece \(-2\,\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w\) contributes \(-2\begin{bmatrix}6&7\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-12&-14\end{bmatrix}\): the constant terms of \(\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}/\partial w_0=6w_0+6w_1-12\) and \(\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}/\partial w_1=6w_0+10w_1-14\).

Rule 2: the statement, and multiplying out \(\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w\)

Overall goal: prove \(\nabla\mathrm{SSE}=-2\,\mathbf r^\top X\) for any number of features.

Look first: L09’s partial derivatives of the quadratic part of our error, \(3w_0^2+6w_0w_1+5w_1^2=\mathbf w^\top X^\top X\mathbf w\), are \(\begin{bmatrix}6w_0+6w_1&6w_0+10w_1\end{bmatrix}=2\,\mathbf w^\top X^\top X\), with \(X^\top X=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{smallmatrix}\right]\) symmetric.

Rule 2 (MML (5.107), p.158: for any square \(B\) the derivative of \(\mathbf w^\top B\mathbf w\) is \(\mathbf w^\top(B+B^\top)\); here \(B\) is symmetric). For a symmetric \(d\times d\) matrix \(A\) (that is, \(A^\top=A\)): \(\;\dfrac{\partial(\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w)}{\partial\mathbf w}=2\,\mathbf w^\top A\). [MASTER]

Proof for two parameters, step 1: multiply out. Take \(A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\\beta&\gamma\end{smallmatrix}\right]\) (symmetric: the same \(\beta\) twice), \(\mathbf w=(w_0,w_1)^\top\).

  • Matrix times column: \(\;A\mathbf w=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha w_0+\beta w_1\\\beta w_0+\gamma w_1\end{bmatrix}\). Then row \(\mathbf w^\top\) times the column \(A\mathbf w\): \[\begin{aligned}\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w&=w_0(\alpha w_0+\beta w_1)+w_1(\beta w_0+\gamma w_1)\\&=\alpha w_0^2+\beta w_0w_1+\beta w_0w_1+\gamma w_1^2=\alpha w_0^2+2\beta\,w_0w_1+\gamma w_1^2 .\end{aligned}\]
  • One \(\beta w_0w_1\) came from the row \(\mathbf w^\top\) on the left of \(A\), the other from the column \(\mathbf w\) on its right.

Rule 2, step 2: differentiate one parameter at a time

Goal: \(\partial(\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w)/\partial\mathbf w=2\,\mathbf w^\top A\) for \(A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\\beta&\gamma\end{smallmatrix}\right]\). Step 1 gave \(\;\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w=\alpha w_0^2+2\beta\,w_0w_1+\gamma w_1^2\). Differentiate each of its three terms, holding the other parameter fixed:

\(\alpha w_0^2\)\(2\beta\,w_0w_1\)\(\gamma w_1^2\)Sum
\(\partial/\partial w_0\)\(2\alpha w_0\)\(2\beta w_1\)\(0\)\(2\alpha w_0+2\beta w_1\)
\(\partial/\partial w_1\)\(0\)\(2\beta w_0\)\(2\gamma w_1\)\(2\beta w_0+2\gamma w_1\)
  • Now compute \(2\,\mathbf w^\top A\), row times each column of \(A\): \(\;\mathbf w^\top A=\begin{bmatrix}w_0&w_1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\\beta&\gamma\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha w_0+\beta w_1&\beta w_0+\gamma w_1\end{bmatrix}\).
  • Times \(2\): \(\;2\,\mathbf w^\top A=\begin{bmatrix}2\alpha w_0+2\beta w_1&2\beta w_0+2\gamma w_1\end{bmatrix}\) — the row of the two sums in the table ✓.
  • Where the \(2\) comes from: \(\mathbf w\) appears on both sides of \(A\). Varying the column \(\mathbf w\) on the right gives \(\mathbf w^\top A\); varying the row \(\mathbf w^\top\) on the left gives \(\mathbf w^\top A^\top\). Symmetry makes \(A^\top=A\), so \(\mathbf w^\top A+\mathbf w^\top A^\top=2\,\mathbf w^\top A\). (For \(d\) parameters: the same computation, longer; MML (5.107).)
Both rules on our error \(\mathrm{SSE}=\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w-2\,\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w+14\), \(A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{smallmatrix}\right]\), \(\mathbf b=(6,7)^\top\): \(\;2\,\mathbf w^\top A-2\,\mathbf b^\top=\begin{bmatrix}6w_0+6w_1-12&6w_0+10w_1-14\end{bmatrix}\), L09’s two partial derivatives ✓.
"Do not say 'each parameter appears twice'. The reason for the 2 is that w sits on both sides of A, and symmetry makes the two contributions equal. For a non-symmetric B the two contributions are w^T B and w^T B^T, which is MML (5.107)."

Assemble the gradient, for any number of features

Start from \(\;\mathrm{SSE}(\mathbf w)=\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w-2\,\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w+\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y\), with \(A=X^\top X\) (symmetric) and \(\mathbf b=X^\top\mathbf y\).
Rule 1: \(\partial(\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w)/\partial\mathbf w=\mathbf b^\top\).   Rule 2 (\(A\) symmetric): \(\partial(\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w)/\partial\mathbf w=2\,\mathbf w^\top A\).

\[\begin{aligned} \nabla\mathrm{SSE}&=\frac{\partial(\mathbf w^\top A\mathbf w)}{\partial\mathbf w}-2\,\frac{\partial(\mathbf b^\top\mathbf w)}{\partial\mathbf w}+\frac{\partial(\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y)}{\partial\mathbf w}&&\text{a sum is differentiated piece by piece}\\ &=2\,\mathbf w^\top A-2\,\mathbf b^\top+\mathbf 0^\top&&\text{Rule 2, Rule 1, and }\mathbf y^\top\mathbf y\text{ has no }\mathbf w\\ &=2\,\mathbf w^\top X^\top X-2\,\mathbf y^\top X&&A=X^\top X,\ \ \mathbf b^\top=(X^\top\mathbf y)^\top=\mathbf y^\top X\\ &=-2\,\big(\mathbf y^\top-\mathbf w^\top X^\top\big)X&&\text{factor out }-2\text{, and }X\text{ on the right}\\ &=-2\,(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)^\top X=-2\,\mathbf r^\top X&&(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)^\top=\mathbf y^\top-\mathbf w^\top X^\top \end{aligned}\]
  • Shapes. \(\mathbf w^\top X^\top X\): \((1\times d)(d\times d)=1\times d\). \(\;\mathbf y^\top X\) and \(\mathbf r^\top X\): \((1\times n)(n\times d)=1\times d\). Every line is a row of \(d\) numbers, as a gradient must be (MML (5.40)). Ours: \((1\times3)(3\times2)=1\times2\).
  • Entry by entry: \(\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}/\partial w_j=-2\times(\text{the column of }X\text{ that multiplies }w_j)^\top\mathbf r\), the shape L09 found one column at a time. Ours, columns \(\mathbf 1=(1,1,1)^\top\) and \(\mathbf t=(0,1,2)^\top\): \(\;\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}/\partial w_0=-2\,\mathbf 1^\top\mathbf r=-2(r_1+r_2+r_3)\) and \(\;\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}/\partial w_1=-2\,\mathbf t^\top\mathbf r=-2(0\cdot r_1+1\cdot r_2+2\cdot r_3)\).
  • MML (5.108): \(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf s}(\mathbf x-A\mathbf s)^\top W(\mathbf x-A\mathbf s)=-2(\mathbf x-A\mathbf s)^\top WA\) for symmetric \(W\). With \(W=I\) and the book’s \(\mathbf x,A,\mathbf s\) read as our \(\mathbf y,X,\mathbf w\), it says \(\nabla\mathrm{SSE}=-2(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)^\top X\).

\(\nabla\mathrm{SSE}=-2\,\mathbf r^\top X\): SSE versus MSE, and where the minus sign comes from

\[\boxed{\;\nabla\mathrm{SSE}=-2\,\mathbf r^\top X\;}\qquad\text{a }1\times d\text{ row, for any number of features.}\] [MASTER]
  • SSE or MSE? Most machine-learning software reports \(\mathrm{MSE}=\mathrm{SSE}/n\). Dividing a function by \(n\) divides its gradient by \(n\): \(\;\nabla\mathrm{MSE}=-\tfrac2n\,\mathbf r^\top X\), the row \(-2\,\mathbf r^\top X\) divided by \(n\). At \((0,0)^\top\), where \(\mathbf r=\mathbf y=(1,3,2)^\top\) and \(-2\,\mathbf r^\top X=\begin{bmatrix}-12&-14\end{bmatrix}\), with \(n=3\): \(\;\nabla\mathrm{MSE}=\begin{bmatrix}-12&-14\end{bmatrix}/3=\begin{bmatrix}-4&-\tfrac{14}{3}\end{bmatrix}\). MSE and SSE are flat at the same \(\mathbf w\).
  • The minus sign. The residual is data minus prediction, \(r_i=y_i-\hat y_i\), and the data \(y_i\) does not move. So \(\partial r_i/\partial\hat y_i=-1\): raise a prediction and its residual falls by the same amount. At \((0,0)^\top\), student 2 has \(y_2=3\); raising \(\hat y_2\) from \(0\) to \(0.1\) moves \(r_2\) from \(3-0=3\) to \(3-0.1=2.9\).
  • ⚠️ Not “increasing a parameter decreases the residual”, which is false. From \((0,0)^\top\) raise \(w_1\) to \(0.1\): predictions \(0.1\,t_i\), with \(\mathbf t=(0,1,2)^\top\), are \((0,\,0.1,\,0.2)^\top\); residuals from \(\mathbf y=(1,3,2)^\top\) are \((1,\,2.9,\,1.8)^\top\). \(r_1\) did not move: student 1 has \(t_1=0\).
  • Per student, \(\frac{d}{d\hat y_i}(y_i-\hat y_i)^2=2r_i\cdot(-1)=-2r_i\) (Calculus 1 chain rule). The factor \(\partial r_i/\partial\hat y_i=-1\) reappears in L13 as one link of the chain.

Set \(\nabla\mathrm{SSE}\) to zero: the normal equation in one line

\[\begin{aligned}\nabla\mathrm{SSE}=\mathbf 0^\top &\overset{(1)}{\iff}-2\,\mathbf r^\top X=\mathbf 0^\top \overset{(2)}{\iff}\mathbf r^\top X=\mathbf 0^\top \overset{(3)}{\iff}X^\top\mathbf r=\mathbf 0\\ &\overset{(4)}{\iff}X^\top(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)=\mathbf 0 \overset{(5)}{\iff}X^\top\mathbf y-X^\top X\mathbf w=\mathbf 0 \overset{(6)}{\iff}X^\top X\mathbf w=X^\top\mathbf y\end{aligned}\]
  1. Substitute \(\nabla\mathrm{SSE}=-2\,\mathbf r^\top X\). The gradient is a row, so “zero” means the zero row \(\mathbf 0^\top\) (\(1\times d\)).
  2. Multiply both sides by \(-\tfrac12\). Reversible: multiply by \(-2\) to go back.
  3. Transpose both sides: \((\mathbf r^\top X)^\top=X^\top\mathbf r\) and \((\mathbf 0^\top)^\top=\mathbf 0\), a \(d\times1\) column. Reversible: transpose again.
  4. Write out the residual, \(\mathbf r=\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\).
  5. Multiply \(X^\top\) into the bracket: \(X^\top(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)=X^\top\mathbf y-X^\top X\mathbf w\).
  6. Add \(X^\top X\mathbf w\) to both sides.

Two routes to \(X^\top X\mathbf w=X^\top\mathbf y\)

  • The normal-equation chain passes through \(X^\top\mathbf r=\mathbf 0\) (\(\mathbf r=\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\)); entry \(j\) says (column \(j\) of \(X\))\(^\top\mathbf r=0\). The residual is orthogonal to every column of \(X\).
  • Route 1, geometry (Unit 2, L04). The closest \(X\mathbf w\) to \(\mathbf y\) is the projection of \(\mathbf y\) onto the columns of \(X\), so \(X^\top\mathbf r=\mathbf 0\); substituting \(\mathbf r=\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\) gave \(X^\top X\mathbf w=X^\top\mathbf y\).
  • Route 2, calculus (this unit). Ask where the error stops changing: \(\nabla\mathrm{SSE}=-2\,\mathbf r^\top X=\mathbf 0^\top\). Dividing by \(-2\) and transposing gives \(X^\top\mathbf r=\mathbf 0\), the geometry route’s starting point.
  • On our data (\(\mathbf r^\ast=\left(-\tfrac12,1,-\tfrac12\right)^\top\) at \(\mathbf w^\ast\); columns \(\mathbf 1=(1,1,1)^\top\), \(\mathbf t=(0,1,2)^\top\)):
    • \(\mathbf 1^\top\mathbf r^\ast=-\tfrac12+1-\tfrac12=0\): the residuals add to zero; the miss above the line, \(+1\), cancels the two below.
    • \(\mathbf t^\top\mathbf r^\ast=0\cdot(-\tfrac12)+1\cdot1+2\cdot(-\tfrac12)=0\): weighted by hours, the residuals add to zero too.
  • The derivation of \(\nabla\mathrm{SSE}=-2\,\mathbf r^\top X\) never used \(d=2\) (only our Rule 2 proof did; MML (5.107) covers every \(d\)), so the same line works for \(50\) features.
Geometry (the residual is orthogonal to the columns) and calculus (the error stops changing) give the same equation \(X^\top X\mathbf w=X^\top\mathbf y\). Two routes, one equation — the milestone of Unit 4.

L13 — A Model Is a Chain

Reading: MML §5.1.2, p.145 (eq. (5.32), the chain rule; Example 5.5, pp.145–146) and §5.2.2, p.148.

The error, computed in stages

Why stages: L12 got \(\nabla\mathrm{SSE}\) by expanding \(\mathrm{SSE}\) into matrix pieces. A neural network is dozens of stages, one after another, and cannot be expanded that way. We need the derivative from the stages, one at a time.

Split the computation of the error into stages, parameters \(\to\) predictions \(\to\) residuals \(\to\) error, with a name on every arrow. For one student \(i\), in L09’s one-parameter model \(\hat y_i=wt_i\):

\[w\;\xrightarrow{\ \times t_i\ }\;\hat y_i\;\xrightarrow{\ y_i-\;\cdot\ }\;r_i\;\xrightarrow{\ (\cdot)^2\ }\;e_i=r_i^2\]
  • Stage 1, prediction: \(\hat y_i=w\,t_i\) — the parameter \(w\) times student \(i\)’s hours \(t_i\).
  • Stage 2, residual: \(r_i=y_i-\hat y_i\) — data minus prediction.
  • Stage 3, squared residual: \(e_i=r_i^2\) — student \(i\)’s share of the error.
  • Our three students, as observations \((t_i,y_i)\): \((0,1)\), \((1,3)\), \((2,2)\) — so \(\mathbf t=(0,1,2)^\top\) hours, \(\mathbf y=(1,3,2)^\top\) points.
  • The error adds the shares: \(\mathrm{SSE}(w)=e_1+e_2+e_3=(1-0\cdot w)^2+(3-1\cdot w)^2+(2-2w)^2\)
  • Expanded, as in L09: \(\;1+(9-6w+w^2)+(4-8w+4w^2)=5w^2-14w+14\), so \(\dfrac{d\,\mathrm{SSE}}{dw}=10w-14\).
"Same three students, same one-parameter model as L09 (y-hat = w t, SSE = 5w^2 - 14w + 14). The only new thing is that we stop jumping from w straight to the error and look at every stage in between. Each arrow is one stage."

One derivative per stage

Each stage is a one-variable function. The data \(t_i\) and \(y_i\) are fixed numbers, so \(t_i\) and \(y_i\) act as constants.

  • Stage 1: \(\hat y_i=t_i\cdot w\) is a line in \(w\) with slope \(t_i\), so \(\;\dfrac{d\hat y_i}{dw}=t_i\).
  • Stage 2: \(r_i=y_i-\hat y_i\). The constant \(y_i\) contributes \(0\), the term \(-\hat y_i\) contributes \(-1\): \(\;\dfrac{dr_i}{d\hat y_i}=0-1=-1\).
  • Stage 3: \(e_i=r_i^2\). Power rule: \(\;\dfrac{de_i}{dr_i}=2r_i\).
  • Read stage 2’s derivative \(-1\): raising a prediction by \(1\) lowers its residual by exactly \(1\). That \(-1\) is where the minus sign in L12’s \(\nabla\mathrm{SSE}=-2\,\mathbf r^\top X\) comes from.
How do the three stage derivatives \(t_i\), \(-1\), \(2r_i\) combine into \(\dfrac{de_i}{dw}\), the rate at which student \(i\)’s squared residual changes when \(w\) changes?

The product of the stage derivatives is the derivative. Do rates always multiply along a chain?

The chain rule, and why it is believable

Chain rule (MML eq. (5.32), p.145). If \(v=f(w)\) and \(z=g(v)\), so that \(z=g(f(w))\), then \[\frac{dz}{dw}=g'(f(w))\,f'(w),\qquad\text{written also as}\qquad \frac{dz}{dw}=\frac{dz}{dv}\cdot\frac{dv}{dw},\] with \(\frac{dz}{dv}\) evaluated at \(v=f(w)\). [MASTER]

Formal proof deferred (it is in any calculus text). Here is the first-order reason, one step at a time:

  • Take a small step \(\delta\) in \(w\).
  • First stage \(v=f(w)\): by the first-order prediction \(f(w+\delta)-f(w)\approx f'(w)\,\delta\) (L09’s “Recall, in the language of error”), \(v\) changes by about \(\Delta v\approx f'(w)\,\delta\).
  • Second stage \(z=g(v)\): the first-order prediction again, now for \(g\) and a step \(\Delta v\) in \(v\), gives \(\Delta z\approx g'(v)\,\Delta v\).
  • Substitute \(\Delta v\approx f'(w)\,\delta\) into \(\Delta z\approx g'(v)\,\Delta v\): \(\Delta z\approx g'(v)\,f'(w)\,\delta\). Change in \(z\) per unit change in \(w\): \(g'(v)\,f'(w)\). Rates multiply along the chain \(w\to v\to z\).
"MML p.148 warns in the margin: dz/dv times dv/dw looks like fractions cancelling, but a derivative is not a fraction. The notation is a memory aid; the first-order argument is the reason."

The chain rule on plain numbers first (MML Example 5.5)

\(h(x)=(2x+1)^4\). Inner stage \(f(x)=2x+1\); outer stage \(g(f)=f^4\).

  • At \(x=1\), the inner value is \(f(1)=2\cdot1+1=3\).

  • Outer, actual: \(3.02^4-3^4=83.1817-81=2.1817\). Close, not equal: the prediction \(2.16\) is only first-order.

Three stages: use the chain rule twice

Our chain \(w\to\hat y_i\to r_i\to e_i\) (\(\hat y_i=wt_i\), \(r_i=y_i-\hat y_i\), \(e_i=r_i^2\)) has three stages, with stage derivatives \(\frac{d\hat y_i}{dw}=t_i\), \(\frac{dr_i}{d\hat y_i}=-1\), \(\frac{de_i}{dr_i}=2r_i\). The chain rule (MML (5.32)) covers two stages, \(v=f(w)\) and \(z=g(v)\). Apply the chain rule twice.

  • \(e_i\) depends on \(r_i\), and \(r_i\) depends on \(w\). Chain rule once: \(\;\dfrac{de_i}{dw}=\dfrac{de_i}{dr_i}\cdot\dfrac{dr_i}{dw}\).
  • \(r_i\) depends on \(\hat y_i\), and \(\hat y_i\) depends on \(w\). Chain rule again: \(\;\dfrac{dr_i}{dw}=\dfrac{dr_i}{d\hat y_i}\cdot\dfrac{d\hat y_i}{dw}\).
  • Substitute the formula for \(\frac{dr_i}{dw}\) into the formula for \(\frac{de_i}{dw}\): \(\;\dfrac{de_i}{dw}=\dfrac{de_i}{dr_i}\cdot\dfrac{dr_i}{d\hat y_i}\cdot\dfrac{d\hat y_i}{dw}\) — one factor per stage.
  • Put in the three stage derivatives \(2r_i\), \(-1\), \(t_i\): \(\;\dfrac{de_i}{dw}=2r_i\cdot(-1)\cdot t_i\).
  • Multiply: \(\;\dfrac{de_i}{dw}=-2\,t_i\,r_i\). [MASTER]
  • The last two factors multiplied, \(2r_i\cdot(-1)=-2r_i\), are L09’s recall fact \(\frac{d}{dx}(c-x)^2=-2r\). The parameter adds the first factor \(t_i\).

  • Student 1, stage 1 flat: \(t_1=0\) hours, so \(\hat y_1=w\cdot0\) does not move when \(w\) moves. \(\frac{de_1}{dw}=2\cdot(-1)\cdot0=0\), even though \(r_1=1\neq0\).
  • Student 3, stage 3 flat: \(r_3=0\), so \(\frac{de_3}{dr_3}=2\cdot0=0\). \(\frac{de_3}{dw}=0\cdot(-1)\cdot2=0\), even though \(t_3=2\neq0\).
  • Either way, the student with a flat stage (student 1 or student 3) contributes \(0\) to \(\frac{d\,\mathrm{SSE}}{dw}\) at \(w=1\).
  • Differentiating \(\mathrm{SSE}(w)=5w^2-14w+14\) gives \(10w-14\), which at \(w=1\) is \(-4=0+(-4)+0\), but cannot say which student or which stage made each term \(0\). The chain rule, stage by stage, can.
"Two different reasons for a zero: student 1 is flat at the first stage (w cannot move that prediction), student 3 at the last stage (a zero residual, where the square has slope zero). The product does not care which factor is the zero."

A fixed bound \(c<1\) makes the product shrink exponentially

By the chain rule, a chain of \(k\) stages has a derivative that is a product of \(k\) stage derivatives. If all \(k\) factors have size at most one fixed \(c<1\) — every factor \(0.9\), say — the product is at most \(c^k\) and shrinks fast.

  • Row \(k=50\): with \(50\) factors of \(0.9\), a small step \(\delta\) at the input of a \(50\)-stage chain moves its last output by only about \(0.005\,\delta\).
  • So the derivative of the error with respect to a parameter at the start of a long chain can be nearly \(0\), and the update to that parameter is tiny, so it barely moves.
  • This scalar chain is the simplest version of the vanishing-gradient problem of deep networks, whose stages are vectors: L14 replaces the scalar factors by Jacobians. [UNDERSTAND]

What the chain rule shows: change one stage, change one factor [UNDERSTAND]

A separate example, same data \((t_i,y_i)=(0,1),(1,3),(2,2)\): swap the model for \(\hat y_i=e^{wt_i}\), so \(r_i=y_i-e^{wt_i}\), \(e_i=r_i^2\).

  • Stage 1 changes. \(\hat y_i=e^{wt_i}\) is itself a two-stage chain: inner \(u=t_iw\) with \(\frac{du}{dw}=t_i\); outer \(\hat y_i=e^{u}\) with \(\frac{d\hat y_i}{du}=e^{u}\).
  • So \(\;\dfrac{d\hat y_i}{dw}=e^{u}\cdot t_i=t_i\,e^{wt_i}\).
  • Stage 2 unchanged: \(\frac{dr_i}{d\hat y_i}=-1\). Stage 3 unchanged: \(\frac{de_i}{dr_i}=2r_i\).
  • New product: \(\;\dfrac{de_i}{dw}=2r_i\cdot(-1)\cdot t_ie^{wt_i}=-2\,t_i\,r_i\,e^{wt_i}\).
  • Against \(-2\,t_i\,r_i\) for the model \(\hat y_i=wt_i\): one extra factor \(e^{wt_i}\), coming from stage 1, the only stage that changed.
  • Here \(\mathrm{SSE}(w)=(1-e^{0\cdot w})^2+(3-e^{w})^2+(2-e^{2w})^2=0+(3-e^{w})^2+(2-e^{2w})^2\) is not a polynomial in \(w\), so there is no polynomial to expand. The chain rule works anyway.
  • In numbers, at \(w=0\): every prediction is \(e^{0}=1\), so \(\mathbf r=(0,2,1)^\top\) and \(\frac{d\,\mathrm{SSE}}{dw}=\sum_i2r_i\cdot(-1)\cdot t_ie^{0}=0-4-4=-8\).

L14 — When a Stage Is a Vector

Reading: MML §5.3, pp.149–154 (Definition 5.6, the Jacobian, p.150; Example 5.9, p.153; Example 5.11, p.154) and §5.2.2, p.148 (the chain rule (5.48)).

"L13 followed one student at a time. Today the three students move together, as one vector, and the derivative of a vector is a matrix. By the end the chain rule rebuilds L12's gradient as a product of three matrices."

The prediction stage is a vector-valued function

Back to two parameters, \(\mathbf w=(w_0,w_1)^\top\). L13 followed one student at a time, but the prediction stage \(\hat{\mathbf y}(\mathbf w)=X\mathbf w\) produces all students’ predictions at once: \(2\) numbers in, \(3\) out, a vector-valued function \(\R^2\to\R^3\) (MML §5.3, eq. (5.54), p.149). Row by row:

\[\hat{\mathbf y}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}w_0\\w_1\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}1\cdot w_0+0\cdot w_1\\1\cdot w_0+1\cdot w_1\\1\cdot w_0+2\cdot w_1\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}w_0\\w_0+w_1\\w_0+2w_1\end{bmatrix}.\]
  • \(\hat y_1=w_0\): student 1 studied \(0\) hours, so the prediction is the intercept alone.
  • \(\hat y_2=w_0+w_1\) (1 hour) and \(\hat y_3=w_0+2w_1\) (2 hours).
  • Nudge each parameter (the figure, from \(\left(\tfrac32,\tfrac12\right)^\top\)): raising \(w_0\) by \(\tfrac12\) moves the three predictions by \(\left(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12\right)^\top\); raising \(w_1\) by \(\tfrac12\) moves them by \(\left(0,\tfrac12,1\right)^\top\). Per unit of nudge: \((1,1,1)^\top\) and \((0,1,2)^\top\).
Each of \(3\) predictions responds to each of \(2\) parameters: six partial derivatives; with \(1000\) students and \(50\) parameters, \(50{,}000\). L13 had one number in and one out at every arrow. How do we arrange them so that the chain rule still works?
"Do the matrix-vector product with them, row times column, before anything else. The point of the third bullet: the six sensitivities are not all the same, and some are zero."

The Jacobian: every partial derivative, in one matrix

Definition (MML Def. 5.6, (5.58)–(5.59), p.150). For \(\mathbf f:\R^n\to\R^m\) with outputs \(f_1,\dots,f_m\) and inputs \(x_1,\dots,x_n\), the Jacobian \(J_{\mathbf f}\), also written \(\dfrac{d\mathbf f}{d\mathbf x}\), is the \(m\times n\) matrix \[J_{\mathbf f}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n}\\ \vdots&&\vdots\\ \dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n}\end{bmatrix},\qquad (i,j)\text{ entry }=\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}:\] one row per output, one column per input. [MASTER]
  • Each entry is a partial derivative (L09, MML Def. 5.5): differentiate the output \(f_i\) in the input \(x_j\), with the other inputs held fixed.
  • Row \(i\) lists how the one output \(f_i\) responds to each input. Column \(j\) lists how every output responds to the one input \(x_j\).
  • The entries are functions of \(\mathbf x\). To get numbers, evaluate the Jacobian at a point.
"MML calls this arrangement numerator layout (Remark, p.150): the outputs label the rows, the inputs label the columns. Say the sentence 'one row per output, one column per input' twice; everything later depends on it."

The Jacobian of the prediction stage is \(X\)

The stage: \(\hat{\mathbf y}=X\mathbf w\), where \(X\) has rows \((1,0),\,(1,1),\,(1,2)\), \(\mathbf w=(w_0,w_1)^\top\). Inputs \(w_0,w_1\) (\(n=2\)), outputs \(\hat y_1,\hat y_2,\hat y_3\) (\(m=3\)), so \(J_{\hat{\mathbf y}}\) is \(3\times2\). One row per prediction:

  • Row 1, \(\hat y_1=w_0\): \(\;\partial\hat y_1/\partial w_0=1\), and \(\partial\hat y_1/\partial w_1=0\) because \(w_1\) does not appear.
  • Row 2, \(\hat y_2=w_0+w_1\): \(\;\partial\hat y_2/\partial w_0=1+0=1\), \(\;\partial\hat y_2/\partial w_1=0+1=1\).
  • Row 3, \(\hat y_3=w_0+2w_1\): \(\;\partial\hat y_3/\partial w_0=1+0=1\), \(\;\partial\hat y_3/\partial w_1=0+2=2\).
\[J_{\hat{\mathbf y}}=\frac{d\hat{\mathbf y}}{d\mathbf w}=\begin{bmatrix} \partial\hat y_1/\partial w_0&\partial\hat y_1/\partial w_1\\ \partial\hat y_2/\partial w_0&\partial\hat y_2/\partial w_1\\ \partial\hat y_3/\partial w_0&\partial\hat y_3/\partial w_1\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}=X .\]
  • No \(w_0\) or \(w_1\) is left in any entry, so \(J_{\hat{\mathbf y}}=X\) at every \(\mathbf w\). Any \(X\) works the same way: \(\hat y_i=\sum_j X_{ij}w_j\), so \(\partial\hat y_i/\partial w_j=X_{ij}\), the entry of \(X\) in row \(i\), column \(j\) (MML Example 5.9, p.153).
The Jacobian of the prediction stage is \(X\) itself — the matrix students have been carrying since Week 2. Nothing new had to be built. [MASTER]
"The six sensitivities of the first slide are the six entries of X. The zero in row 1 is the student who studied 0 hours: the slope parameter cannot reach that prediction."

Quick check, separate example: a Jacobian computed entry by entry

A separate example, not our data: \(\;\mathbf f(x_1,x_2)=\begin{bmatrix}x_1^2x_2\\3x_1+x_2^2\end{bmatrix}\), a function \(\R^2\to\R^2\), so \(J_{\mathbf f}\) is \(2\times2\).

  • \(\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1}\): hold \(x_2\) fixed, so \(x_1^2x_2\) is \(x_2\) times \(x_1^2\), and the derivative is \(x_2\cdot2x_1=2x_1x_2\).
  • \(\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_2}\): hold \(x_1\) fixed, so \(x_1^2x_2\) is the constant \(x_1^2\) times \(x_2\), and the derivative is \(x_1^2\cdot1=x_1^2\).
  • \(\dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_1}\): \(3x_1\) gives \(3\); \(x_2^2\) is held fixed, so it gives \(0\). Total \(3+0=3\).
  • \(\dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_2}\): \(3x_1\) is held fixed, so it gives \(0\); \(x_2^2\) gives \(2x_2\). Total \(0+2x_2=2x_2\).
\[J_{\mathbf f}=\begin{bmatrix}2x_1x_2&x_1^2\\3&2x_2\end{bmatrix},\qquad J_{\mathbf f}(1,2)=\begin{bmatrix}2\cdot1\cdot2&1^2\\3&2\cdot2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\3&4\end{bmatrix}.\]

The output at \((1,2)^\top\): \(\mathbf f(1,2)=\big(1^2\cdot2,\;3\cdot1+2^2\big)^\top=(2,\,7)^\top\).

"Row 1 came from f1 only, row 2 from f2 only. Column 1 came from differentiating in x1 only. Have them say which entry each bullet fills before it appears."

The residual stage: its Jacobian is \(-I\)

The stage \(\mathbf r=\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}\) (data minus prediction) takes the \(3\) predictions to the \(3\) residuals: \(\R^3\to\R^3\), so its Jacobian is \(3\times3\). The data \(\mathbf y=(1,3,2)^\top\) are fixed numbers.

  • Written out: \(\;r_1=1-\hat y_1,\quad r_2=3-\hat y_2,\quad r_3=2-\hat y_3\).
  • Diagonal entries: \(\partial r_i/\partial\hat y_i=0-1=-1\), the constant \(y_i\) giving \(0\). The diagonal \(-1\) is L13's \(dr_i/d\hat y_i=-1\).
  • Off-diagonal entries: \(r_1=1-\hat y_1\) contains no \(\hat y_2\) and no \(\hat y_3\), so \(\partial r_1/\partial\hat y_2=\partial r_1/\partial\hat y_3=0\). Likewise \(r_2\) contains only \(\hat y_2\), and \(r_3\) only \(\hat y_3\).
\[J_{\mathbf r}=\frac{d\mathbf r}{d\hat{\mathbf y}}=\begin{bmatrix} \partial r_1/\partial\hat y_1&\partial r_1/\partial\hat y_2&\partial r_1/\partial\hat y_3\\ \partial r_2/\partial\hat y_1&\partial r_2/\partial\hat y_2&\partial r_2/\partial\hat y_3\\ \partial r_3/\partial\hat y_1&\partial r_3/\partial\hat y_2&\partial r_3/\partial\hat y_3\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}-1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&-1\end{bmatrix}=-I .\]
  • Like the prediction stage's Jacobian \(X\), the Jacobian \(-I\) is the same at every point: no \(\hat y_i\) is left in it.
  • The six zeros say something L13 never had to say: one student's residual does not respond to another student's prediction.
"The minus sign in -2 r^T X lives on the diagonal here. The zeros are why the students separate cleanly."

The error stage: one output, so one row

The chain so far: \(\hat{\mathbf y}=X\mathbf w\), \(\;\mathbf r=\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}\) with \(\mathbf y=(1,3,2)^\top\). The last stage is the error \(\mathrm{SSE}=\mathbf r^\top\mathbf r=r_1^2+r_2^2+r_3^2\). It takes the \(3\) residuals to \(1\) number: \(\R^3\to\R\), so its Jacobian is \(1\times3\).

  • \(\dfrac{\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}}{\partial r_1}\): \(r_2^2\) and \(r_3^2\) are held fixed and give \(0\); \(r_1^2\) gives \(2r_1\). Total \(2r_1+0+0=2r_1\).
  • \(\dfrac{\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}}{\partial r_2}=0+2r_2+0=2r_2\), \(\qquad\dfrac{\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}}{\partial r_3}=0+0+2r_3=2r_3\).
\[J_{\mathrm{SSE}}=\frac{d\,\mathrm{SSE}}{d\mathbf r}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}}{\partial r_1}&\dfrac{\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}}{\partial r_2}&\dfrac{\partial\,\mathrm{SSE}}{\partial r_3}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2r_1&2r_2&2r_3\end{bmatrix}=2\,\mathbf r^\top .\]
  • Each entry is L13's \(de_i/dr_i=2r_i\) for one student, now collected into one row.
  • Unlike \(X\) (prediction stage) and \(-I\) (residual stage), the Jacobian \(2\mathbf r^\top\) changes from point to point. At \(\mathbf w=(0,0)^\top\) the residual is \(\mathbf r=\mathbf y-X\mathbf 0=(1,3,2)^\top\), and there it is \(\begin{bmatrix}2\cdot1&2\cdot3&2\cdot2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&6&4\end{bmatrix}\).
A function with one output has a Jacobian with one row. A row of partial derivatives of one output already has a name in this course.
"MML (5.81), p.154 is exactly this line: the derivative of the squared norm of a vector is two times that vector, transposed."

The chain rule, matrix form

Chain rule, matrix form (MML (5.48), p.148). [MASTER] Let \(\mathbf g:\R^n\to\R^k\) be differentiable at \(\mathbf w\), and \(\mathbf f:\R^k\to\R^m\) differentiable at \(\mathbf g(\mathbf w)\). Then \[J_{\mathbf f\circ\mathbf g}(\mathbf w)=J_{\mathbf f}\big(\mathbf g(\mathbf w)\big)\;J_{\mathbf g}(\mathbf w),\qquad\text{sizes: }(m\times n)=(m\times k)(k\times n).\]
  • Stated, not proved here. On our three-stage model the product \((2\mathbf r^\top)(-I)X\) gave \(\begin{bmatrix}-12&-14\end{bmatrix}\) at \((0,0)^\top\); the next slides check it at every \(\mathbf w\).
  • \(J_{\mathbf f}\) is evaluated at \(\mathbf g(\mathbf w)\), the point the inner stage delivers — not at \(\mathbf w\).
  • The \(k\) columns of \(J_{\mathbf f}\) and the \(k\) rows of \(J_{\mathbf g}\) both belong to the middle variables \(v_1,\dots,v_k\), so the product is defined. Entry \((i,j)\): \(\;\dfrac{\partial(\mathbf f\circ\mathbf g)_i}{\partial w_j}=\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial v_l}\,\frac{\partial v_l}{\partial w_j}\), one product per middle variable, then add.
  • If \(n=k=m=1\), every Jacobian is \(1\times1\) and the rule is L13's \(\;dz/dw=(dz/dv)\cdot(dv/dw)\).
  • L11 used the matrix chain rule without stating it. For \(\phi(h)=g(\mathbf w+h\mathbf u)\), the inner map \(h\mapsto\mathbf w+h\mathbf u\) has Jacobian \(\mathbf u\) (\(2\times1\)) and the outer \(g\) has Jacobian \(\nabla g\) (\(1\times2\)), so \(\phi'(0)=\nabla g(\mathbf w)\,\mathbf u\).
"Same rule as L13, with products of numbers replaced by products of matrices. The sum in the third bullet is the row-times-column rule of matrix multiplication, nothing more. For the L11 bullet: d/dh of (w0 + h u0, w1 + h u1) is (u0, u1)^T = u, and at h = 0 the outer Jacobian is evaluated at w."

Step 2. \((-2\mathbf r^\top)X\): \(\;(1\times3)(3\times2)\to1\times2\), the row times each column of \(X\):

\[\begin{aligned}\begin{bmatrix}-2r_1&-2r_2&-2r_3\end{bmatrix}\!\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix} &=\begin{bmatrix}-2r_1\cdot1-2r_2\cdot1-2r_3\cdot1&\;-2r_1\cdot0-2r_2\cdot1-2r_3\cdot2\end{bmatrix}\\ &=\begin{bmatrix}-2(r_1+r_2+r_3)&-2(r_2+2r_3)\end{bmatrix}.\end{aligned}\]
  • Step 2 is \(-2\,\mathbf r^\top X\): L12's \(\nabla\mathrm{SSE}=-2\,\mathbf r^\top X\), rebuilt one stage at a time, without expanding \(\mathrm{SSE}\). [MASTER]
"Step 1 only flips every sign; the zeros of -I kill every cross term. Step 2 is row times column, twice." MML does the same computation in Example 5.11, (5.81)-(5.83), p.154 (its residual is called e, its matrix Phi).

The row \(-2\mathbf r^\top X\), written in \(w_0\) and \(w_1\)

The product of the three Jacobians gave \(d\,\mathrm{SSE}/d\mathbf w=-2\mathbf r^\top X=\begin{bmatrix}-2(r_1+r_2+r_3)&-2(r_2+2r_3)\end{bmatrix}\). Substitute \(\mathbf r=\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\) with \(\mathbf y=(1,3,2)^\top\), \(X\) with rows \((1,0),\,(1,1),\,(1,2)\), row by row: \(\;r_1=1-w_0,\quad r_2=3-w_0-w_1,\quad r_3=2-w_0-2w_1\).

  • First entry. \(r_1+r_2+r_3=(1+3+2)-(w_0+w_0+w_0)-(0+w_1+2w_1)=6-3w_0-3w_1\).
    Times \(-2\): \(\;-12+6w_0+6w_1\).
  • Second entry. \(r_2+2r_3=(3-w_0-w_1)+(4-2w_0-4w_1)=7-3w_0-5w_1\).
    Times \(-2\): \(\;-14+6w_0+10w_1\).
\[\frac{d\,\mathrm{SSE}}{d\mathbf w}=\begin{bmatrix}6w_0+6w_1-12&6w_0+10w_1-14\end{bmatrix}.\]
  • The two entries \(6w_0+6w_1-12\) and \(6w_0+10w_1-14\) are exactly L09's partial derivatives of \(\mathrm{SSE}(w_0,w_1)=3w_0^2+6w_0w_1+5w_1^2-12w_0-14w_1+14\).
Your turn, at \(\mathbf w^\ast=\left(\tfrac32,\tfrac12\right)^\top\): \(\mathbf r^\ast=\left(-\tfrac12,1,-\tfrac12\right)^\top\), \(2\mathbf r^{\ast\top}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&2&-1\end{bmatrix}\); times \(-I\): \(\begin{bmatrix}1&-2&1\end{bmatrix}\); times \(X\): \(\begin{bmatrix}1-2+1&0-2+2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\end{bmatrix}\) ✓, L10’s lowest point. Only \(2\mathbf r^\top\) changed; \(-I\) and \(X\) are the same at every \(\mathbf w\).

L14 takeaways

  • A stage with several inputs and several outputs is a vector-valued function; its Jacobian holds every partial derivative, one row per output, one column per input (MML Def. 5.6, p.150). [MASTER]
  • Our three stages: prediction \(\hat{\mathbf y}=X\mathbf w\) has Jacobian \(X\); residual \(\mathbf r=\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}\) has \(-I\); error \(\mathrm{SSE}=\mathbf r^\top\mathbf r\) has the one row \(2\mathbf r^\top\). [MASTER]
  • A one-row Jacobian is the gradient: that is why the gradient is a row (MML (5.40), p.146; Remark, p.147). [MASTER]
  • Chain rule, matrix form (MML (5.48), p.148): Jacobians multiply, last stage first. \((2\mathbf r^\top)(-I)X=-2\,\mathbf r^\top X\) rebuilds L12’s gradient: \(\begin{bmatrix}-12&-14\end{bmatrix}\) at \((0,0)^\top\), \(\begin{bmatrix}0&0\end{bmatrix}\) at \(\mathbf w^\ast\). [MASTER]
  • Three routes, one row \(\begin{bmatrix}6w_0+6w_1-12&6w_0+10w_1-14\end{bmatrix}\): the expanded polynomial (L09), two matrix rules (L12), a product of three Jacobians (L14). [MASTER]
