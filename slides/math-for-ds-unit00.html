Part II — Inside ChatGPT

How it picks the next word

ChatGPT plays one game: fill in the blank

When you type "The cat sat on the _", ChatGPT's entire job is to guess the next word.

  • It looks at all the words before the blank.
  • It asks: "Based on these words, what word is most likely next?"

That's literally all it does. It fills in one blank. Then the sentence is longer, so it fills in the NEXT blank. Then the NEXT. One word at a time.

"Here's a secret: ChatGPT doesn't write paragraphs. It writes ONE WORD. Then it reads everything including that new word, and writes the NEXT word. It's like a student writing an essay one word at a time, reading the whole thing before each new word."

Write on the board: "The cat sat on the _" Ask: "What word goes here?" Everyone says "mat" or "floor" or "couch." "How did YOU know? You looked at the words 'cat', 'sat', 'on' and your brain figured out what fits. ChatGPT does the exact same thing — but with arithmetic instead of intuition."

This is the bridge: YOUR brain and ChatGPT are solving the same problem. Your brain uses intuition. ChatGPT uses math. We're going to learn that math.

But how? Four steps of arithmetic.

"The cat sat on the _"

  1. Turn every word into a list of numbers. ("cat" → some numbers, "sat" → some numbers, …)
  2. For each word, ask: how related is it to the blank? (Score each word.)
  3. Turn the scores into percentages. (cat gets 40%, sat gets 30%, the gets 15%, on gets 15%.)
  4. Mix the words' information using those percentages → predict "mat".

That's it. Four steps. Each step is a math concept we'll learn.

Write the four steps on the board. Number them clearly.

"Step 1 is about REPRESENTATION: how do you turn a word into numbers? That's vectors. We learn this Week 1." "Step 2 is about COMPARISON: how do you measure if two things are related? That's the dot product. Also Week 1." "Step 3 is about DECISION: how do you convert 'this is more related than that' into 'give 40% attention here and 15% there'? That's probability. We learn this in Week 12." "Step 4 is about COMBINATION: how do you mix several things together with different weights? That's like computing your GPA. We learn this Week 1."

"Four steps. Four math ideas. All things you can understand by Christmas."

Step 1: Words become numbers

How do you turn "cat" into numbers?

A computer can't read. It only understands numbers. So we need a way to turn every word into numbers.

  • Think of it like a report card for the word.
  • Each number measures one quality: how "animal-like" is this word? How "action-like"?
Word Animal score Action score
cat 4 1
dog 4.5 2
run 0 5
democracy 0 0

"cat" becomes the numbers \((4, 1)\). "dog" becomes \((4.5, 2)\). Now we can do math with words!

The report card analogy is KEY. Every student understands report cards.

"Think of each word getting a report card. But instead of 'Math: B+, English: A-', it's 'Animal: 4, Action: 1.' Cat gets a high animal score and a low action score. Run gets a low animal score and a high action score."

Draw on the board: a 2D coordinate system. Plot the four words.

  • cat at (4, 1)
  • dog at (4.5, 2) — CLOSE to cat
  • run at (0, 5) — far from cat
  • democracy at (0, 0) — far from everything

"Look at the picture. Which words are close together?" Cat and dog. "Which are far apart?" Cat and democracy. "The computer doesn't need to 'understand' language. It just needs to see that cat and dog are NEARBY in number-space."

"In math, we call this list of numbers a VECTOR. A vector is just a list of numbers. That's it. Nothing scary."

"In real AI systems, the list has 768 numbers or more, not 2. But the idea is exactly the same: similar words are nearby."

Step 2: Measuring closeness

How do you measure "is cat more related to dog or to democracy"?

We need a simple rule: given two lists of numbers, output one number that says "how similar are they?"

The rule is called the dot product. It's just: multiply matching numbers and add up.

"cat" \(= (4, 1)\), "dog" \(= (4.5, 2)\):

\[4 \times 4.5\; +\; 1 \times 2\; =\; 18 + 2\; =\; 20 \quad \text{← big number = very similar!}\]

"cat" \(= (4, 1)\), "democracy" \(= (0, 0)\):

\[4 \times 0\; +\; 1 \times 0\; =\; 0 \quad \text{← zero = not related at all}\]

20 vs. 0. The math confirms what you already felt: cat and dog are similar; cat and democracy are not.

DO THIS ON THE BOARD. Slowly. Step by step.

"4 times 4.5 is 18. 1 times 2 is 2. Add them: 20. Big number. Cat and dog are similar." "4 times 0 is 0. 1 times 0 is 0. Add them: 0. Zero. Cat and democracy have nothing in common."

Ask: "Could you do this arithmetic? It's multiplication and addition. Middle school math."

"But here's the amazing part: this middle-school arithmetic is EXACTLY what ChatGPT does trillions of times to understand language. When ChatGPT reads your sentence, it computes this dot product between every pair of words to figure out which words are related."

"The dot product is not fancy math. It's multiply and add. But it's the single most important operation in all of AI."

This is the AI → Math arrow: AI uses the dot product, and that motivates us to understand what the dot product really measures. We'll spend two lectures in Unit 1 going deep on this.

Why does multiply-and-add measure similarity?

Here's the intuition:

  • If both words score high on "animal" (4 and 4.5), the product is a big positive (18).
  • If both words score low on "action" (1 and 2), the product is small but positive (2).
  • If one word scores high and the other scores zero, the product is zero (no agreement).

The dot product adds up all the agreements. More agreement → bigger number → more similar.

This is how ChatGPT decides which words in your sentence are related to each other. It computes this for every pair. That operation is called \(QK^\top\) in the attention formula.

"Why does this work? Because the dot product is adding up how much the two words AGREE on every quality."

"If both words are very 'animal-like', that dimension contributes a big number. If both are very 'action-like', that contributes too. The total is the sum of all their agreements."

"Zero means no agreement. Negative would mean they're OPPOSITE — pointing in different directions."

"This is the deep math intuition: the dot product measures how much two vectors POINT IN THE SAME DIRECTION. We'll make this precise in Unit 1. But for now, just remember: multiply, add, bigger = more similar."

The Math → Real World connection: this simple arithmetic lets Spotify find songs similar to your favorites, lets Google find web pages similar to your search, lets ChatGPT find words similar to your context.

Step 3: Scores become percentages

From "how related" to "how much attention"

After computing dot products, ChatGPT has a score for each word:

Word The cat sat on
Score 2 20 15 5

But these are just numbers. We need percentages: out of 100% of my attention, how much goes to each word?

Word The cat sat on
Attention 5% 47% 36% 12%

"cat" gets 47% of the attention. Makes sense — it's the most important word for predicting what comes next.

"The scores say cat = 20, sat = 15, on = 5, the = 2. But what does '20' mean? Is that a lot? We need context."

"Converting to percentages gives us context. Now we can say: 'almost half the attention goes to cat, about a third to sat, a little to on, barely any to the.' This makes sense — for 'the cat sat on the _', the word 'cat' is the most important clue."

"The math function that converts scores to percentages is called SOFTMAX. It's a little more complicated than simple division (it uses the exponential function), but the idea is the same: turn raw numbers into percentages that add up to 100%."

"We'll learn exactly how softmax works in Week 12 when we study probability. For now, just know: scores go in, percentages come out."

This is where probability enters the picture. The Real World → Math arrow: we need probability because AI needs to make DECISIONS, not just compute scores. Probability tells us HOW MUCH to trust each option.

Step 4: The weighted mix

Mixing information with percentages — like computing your GPA

You all know how GPA works:

Class Grade Credits  
Math 3.5 4 credits 3.5 × 4 = 14.0
English 3.0 3 credits 3.0 × 3 = 9.0
Art 4.0 1 credit 4.0 × 1 = 4.0
Total   8 credits 27.0
GPA     27 ÷ 8 = 3.375
  • 4-credit Math counts more than 1-credit Art. That's a weighted average.
  • The credits are the weights. They decide what matters most.

EVERY student knows GPA. This is the most relatable analogy in the course.

"Your GPA is not a simple average. Math counts 4 times as much as Art because it has 4 credits. That's a weighted average — some things count more."

Do the arithmetic on the board: 3.5 × 4 = 14, 3.0 × 3 = 9, 4.0 × 1 = 4. Total = 27. Divide by 8 credits = 3.375.

"This is middle school arithmetic. Multiply, add, divide."

ChatGPT computes a GPA of words

The attention percentages are like credits — they decide which words count more.

Word Info the word carries Attention (weight)
cat (animal, pet, furry, …) 47%
sat (past tense, position, …) 36%
on (preposition, surface, …) 12%
the (article, …) 5%

Result = 47% × (cat info) + 36% × (sat info) + 12% × (on info) + 5% × (the info)

This is a weighted average of word information. In math, we call it a linear combination. It's the most important operation in linear algebra — and we start learning it Week 1.

"Attention is literally a GPA of words. The weights (47%, 36%, …) are like credits. The word information is like grades. The output is a weighted mix."

Write the formula on the board: output = 0.47 × vcat + 0.36 × vsat + 0.12 × von + 0.05 × vthe

"This is a linear combination. It's the first topic of Unit 1. The entire semester is building up to understanding why this simple operation — multiply and add — is so powerful."

"What makes this powerful is the WEIGHTS. If you change the weights, you change what the model pays attention to. Learning the right weights is what makes ChatGPT smart."

Math → AI: linear combinations are the language of attention. AI → Real World: attention is how ChatGPT understands your sentences. Real World → Math: wanting to understand ChatGPT motivates learning linear algebra. The triangle in action.

One more thing: how does it learn?

The machine tunes itself — like learning to cook

Imagine you're learning to cook rice.

  1. First try: too much water. Mushy. Lesson: use less water.
  2. Second try: too little water. Crunchy. Lesson: use a bit more.
  3. Third try: almost right. Slightly dry. Lesson: just a tiny bit more.
  4. Eventually: perfect rice. You learned by adjusting one thing at a time.

ChatGPT learns the same way — except it adjusts billions of numbers instead of one.

"Learning to cook rice: you try, fail, adjust, try again. Each time, you change ONE thing and see if it gets better."

"ChatGPT has 175 billion numbers to adjust — those are the weights. It makes a prediction. Checks if it's right. If not, it figures out WHICH numbers to change and by HOW MUCH. Then it does it again. Billions of times."

"The math tool for 'which direction to adjust' is called the GRADIENT. It's a concept from calculus. The algorithm for 'keep adjusting until it's good enough' is called GRADIENT DESCENT. These are the topics of Weeks 5-10."

"But notice: the core idea is simple. Try, fail, adjust. You already know this from cooking, from sports, from any skill. The math just makes it precise."

The math of "try, fail, adjust"

  1. Try: The model makes a prediction. ("The next word is 'dog'.")
  2. Fail: We measure how wrong it was. (The answer was "mat", not "dog".)
  3. Adjust: Calculus tells us which numbers to change and by how much.

"How wrong" → this is the loss function (Week 2, Mean Squared Error). "Which direction to change" → this is the gradient (Weeks 5–7). "Keep adjusting" → this is gradient descent (Weeks 8–10).

We won't do this today. But by Week 10, you'll compute a gradient by hand and watch a model learn.

"Three steps. Try, fail, adjust. Each step has a math name."

"Measuring 'how wrong': that's the loss function. The simplest one is Mean Squared Error — take the difference between your answer and the right answer, square it. We'll build one in Week 2."

"Figuring out 'which direction to change': that's the gradient. It's a list of numbers that says 'increase weight #1, decrease weight #2, …' We'll compute gradients starting Week 5."

"Doing the adjusting repeatedly: that's gradient descent. Walk downhill on the error surface. We'll implement it in Weeks 8-10."

"This is the calculus part of the course. Calculus isn't about abstract curves — it's about LEARNING. Every time ChatGPT improves, it's because calculus told it which direction to go."

This drives the AI → Math motivation: "I want to understand how AI LEARNS. For that, I need calculus."

Part III — The Formula

Putting it all together

The attention formula — now you know what it means

\[\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\]

Let's read it left to right, using what we just learned:

  1. \(QK^\top\) → Compute the dot product between every pair of words. (Step 2: how related?)
  2. \(\div\sqrt{d_k}\) → Scale down so the numbers don't get too extreme. (More on this in a moment.)
  3. softmax → Convert scores to percentages. (Step 3: how much attention?)
  4. \(\cdot\; V\) → Take the weighted average. (Step 4: mix the information.)

This is the complete recipe. Everything else in ChatGPT is repetition and plumbing.

"You now know what every piece of this formula does. Let me say that again: you can READ this formula. An hour ago it was hieroglyphics. Now you know what Q, K, V are, what the dot product does, what softmax does, what multiplying by V does."

"We haven't done the math rigorously yet — that's what the rest of the semester is for. But you know the STORY. And in math, knowing the story is half the battle."

Point at each piece on the board as you read it. Make it physical.

Why the \(\div\sqrt{d_k}\)? A quick detour.

What happens if you skip dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\)?

Without scaling: scores \(= [50,\ 10,\ 2]\) → percentages → \([99.9\%,\ 0.1\%,\ 0.0\%]\) With scaling: scores \(= [5,\ 1,\ 0.2]\) → percentages → \([84\%,\ 12\%,\ 4\%]\)

  • Without scaling: the model puts 99.9% on ONE word. It can only "look at" one thing.
  • With scaling: the model spreads attention across several words. Much better.

It's like a student who only reads one textbook chapter vs. one who reads several. The second student understands better.

"When vectors are long (768 dimensions in real GPT), the dot products are huge because you're adding 768 terms. Huge scores go into softmax and softmax turns them into near-100%-or-near-0%."

"That's bad. It means the model can only pay attention to ONE word. But understanding a sentence requires looking at MULTIPLE words."

"Dividing by √dk brings the scores back to a reasonable range. It's like adjusting the volume on headphones — too loud and you hear only the loudest instrument, turn it down and you hear the whole band."

"We'll make this precise in Unit 1 when we talk about norms (measuring the 'size' of a vector). For now, just know: scaling = keeping the model flexible."

The course arc

One problem, five ways

This semester we'll solve the same problem five different times:

How do you fit a line to data?

  1. By geometry (Week 2): Drop a perpendicular. The closest line is the best line.
  2. By algebra (Week 3): Solve a system of equations.
  3. By calculus (Week 5): Take the slope of the error, find where it's zero.
  4. By optimization (Week 8): Start anywhere, walk downhill.
  5. By probability (Week 13): Find the line that best explains the randomness in data.

Five approaches, same answer every time. But each one teaches you something the others can't.

"Why would we solve the same problem five times? Because each approach gives you a different INSIGHT."

"Geometry shows you the SHAPE: the best fit line is the closest one, like dropping a perpendicular." "Algebra shows you the CALCULATION: you can solve it exactly with a formula." "Calculus shows you the SLOPE: you find where the error stops decreasing." "Optimization shows you the PROCESS: how a computer actually finds the answer step by step." "Probability shows you the NOISE: the data has randomness, and the best line is the one that best explains it."

"It's like looking at a building from five angles. Front view, side view, top view, inside view, night view. No single view tells you everything. Together, you understand the whole building."

"And here's the payoff: when you understand regression five ways, you understand HOW ChatGPT learns. Because ChatGPT learning is just regression at scale — fitting a model to data by walking downhill on the error."
