L03 — Projection: The Geometry of "Closest"

Three friends guess your age:

AliceBobCarol
192120
  • There is one unknown — your age. Call it \(x\). Each guess is a claim about that one number: \(x = 19\), \(x = 21\), \(x = 20\). No single number satisfies all three.
  • You cannot be right for everyone. You can only be wrong by as little as possible.
  • You'd probably say the average: \(x^* = \frac{19+21+20}{3} = 20\) — the best compromise.
But why the average? When the answers conflict, what does "best" mean — and how do we find it?
"Start with the simplest possible version: one unknown, three conflicting answers. Everyone's intuition says take the average. The intuition is right — the question is WHY. Answering that gives us a tool that scales all the way to training a neural network." Ask the class for their guess before revealing the average.

Why the average? Minimizing squared error

We never find out your real age. All we have is the three answers — so "error" here does not mean distance from the truth. It means how far our one answer sits from each friend's.

Pick a candidate \(x\). It misses Alice by \(x-19\), Bob by \(x-21\), Carol by \(x-20\). Square each one, so being 2 too high counts the same as being 2 too low. Then add them up:

The average gives the smallest total. Not a coincidence — it's a theorem, and it needed no true answer to find.
"Watch for this question, because it is the right one: how can we talk about error when we never learn the real age? We can't — and we don't have to. The quantity we minimize is disagreement with the data we were given, not distance from a truth we never see. That is exactly the situation in every AI model: you have training data, you never have the true function." "Why square? Two reasons: it stops a +2 and a -2 from cancelling to zero, and it makes a miss of 3 count nine times as much as a miss of 1." "The x here is not a fourth guess — it is our single proposed answer, the one number we have to commit to."

From the average to least squares

The table showed it for five candidates. Here is the claim in full, for any data at all:

Theorem. For any numbers \(b_1,\dots,b_n\), the total squared miss \[f(x) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x - b_i)^2\] is smallest at exactly one value of \(x\): the average \(\;\bar{b} = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i b_i\). Its smallest value is \(\sum_i (\bar{b} - b_i)^2\).
  • Our data: \(\bar b = \frac{19+21+20}{3} = 20\), and the leftover is \(1+1+0 = 2\) — exactly the smallest row of the table.
  • "Exactly one" is part of the claim: no tie, no second-best answer.
  • That leftover \(\sum_i(\bar b - b_i)^2\) is the error no choice of \(x\) can remove. The friends disagree with each other; no single number can fix that.
"State it as a theorem, on the board, before proving anything. Three parts: the minimizer is the average, it is unique, and the leftover has a formula." "Note what the theorem does NOT say: it says nothing about the true age. It is a statement about the numbers we were handed."

Why the average wins

You already know how to minimize a function: differentiate, set it to zero.

\[f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n (x-b_i)^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f'(x)=2\sum_{i=1}^n (x-b_i)=2\Big(nx-\sum_{i=1}^n b_i\Big)\]
  • Set \(f'(x)=0\): \(\;nx=\sum_i b_i\;\), so \(\;x=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i b_i=\bar b\). The average falls out.
  • \(f''(x)=2n>0\) everywhere, so this is a minimum — and it's the only place \(f'\) is zero, so it's the only one.
  • Our data: \(f'(x)=2(3x-60)=0 \Rightarrow x=20\).
This is exactly what training a model does: write down the error, differentiate, and go where the derivative says. Every AI system in this course is doing this — just with many variables instead of one.
"They have Calculus 2, so lead with the derivative — it is the tool they trust, and it is literally what gradient descent does." "Emphasize f'' > 0: it is what upgrades 'critical point' to 'minimum', and it is why least squares has no local-minimum trouble. That single fact is why linear regression is solvable and neural networks are not." "The homework proves the same theorem again with pure algebra. That version reveals structure the derivative hides — the error splits into a piece you control and a piece you cannot — and that split becomes the Pythagorean theorem in L03. Tell them to watch for it." "MML asserts the projection is closest (Ch 3.8, 'using geometric arguments') but never proves it. This proof is ours."

This is how AI models train

Every AI training loop does the same thing:

  1. Make a prediction.
  2. Compare it to the answer recorded in the data.
  3. Measure the error — square it, then add up over all the data.
  4. Adjust to make that total smaller.

Step 2 says recorded in the data — not the truth. A model never sees the truth. It only sees what was written down, and that can be noisy, or disagree with itself, exactly like three friends who give you three different ages.

The age problem is steps 1–3, with the three guesses as the recorded answers. Today we build the geometry of step 4.
"ChatGPT, image generators, self-driving cars — all train this way. Scale and complexity differ; the loop does not." "Be careful with step 2, and say this out loud: we are NOT comparing against truth. We compare against recorded data. A student who asks 'but how do we know the right answer?' has caught the most important thing on this slide — we don't, and we never will. Least squares needs only the recorded answers." "The math of 'adjust' is what this entire unit is about."

Name the pattern: overdetermined system

The age-guessing problem has a famous twin — the one in your pocket:

A system \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) with more equations than unknowns (\(m > n\)) is called overdetermined.

A best-fit solution minimizes \(\norm{A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}}\), where \(\norm{\cdot}\) with no subscript is the Euclidean (\(\ell_2\)) norm — the Unit 1 default, and MML's.
  • GPS. Each satellite reports only how far away it is — that puts you somewhere on a circle around it. Two circles already cross at a point, so with perfect readings two satellites would be enough, and a third would pass through that same point.
  • But every reading carries error, so the three circles bound a small triangle instead of meeting. No point sits on all three. The error causes that — not the extra satellite.
  • So why carry a third? Two satellites give an exact answer that is exactly as wrong as your error, with nothing to warn you. Three cannot all be satisfied — and that failure is the information.
  • \(m > n\) is not the problem; it is the redundancy that beats the noise. Your phone picks the point that misses all three by the least. AI training is the same trade: millions of examples, thousands of weights (\(m \gg n\)).
"Expect the objection, because it is the right one: three circles CAN meet at a point, so m > n does not by itself break anything. Say so plainly — with exact measurements the third satellite is redundant and consistent. Noise is what makes it inconsistent." "Then the inversion, which is the whole slide: redundancy is not the disease, it is the cure. Two satellites hand you a wrong answer with total confidence; three hand you a triangle whose size tells you how wrong you are." "If someone asks what we actually do about the noise — we do not fix the satellites. We pick the point minimising the total squared miss. That is least squares, and it is running in their pocket." "In real AI, you might have a million training examples but only 10,000 weights. That's an overdetermined system with m = 1,000,000 and n = 10,000. The math is the same as our three-friends problem, just bigger."

Quick check

Alice adds a fourth friend's guess: 20.5. Does the best-fit error go up or down compared to three friends?

Up — adding another constraint to satisfy usually increases the total error. More data means more equations to juggle, so the best compromise gets slightly worse per equation.

This is why training error in machine learning usually decreases as you add parameters but increases as you add data — more data means more constraints.
"This is a subtle but important point. More data gives a better model for new predictions (generalization), but the in-sample error on the training data goes up because there are more constraints to satisfy. We'll revisit this tradeoff in Unit 5 when we talk about regularization."

The real question: closest point

Imagine you're standing on a hill and you want to get to a road below. What's the shortest path to the road?

  • Walk straight toward the road — perpendicular.
  • Any other path would be longer.
  • The point where you hit the road is the closest point.
In math: given a point and a line (or subspace), the closest point on the line is the orthogonal projection.
"This everyday intuition — the shortest path is the perpendicular one — is the key geometric insight behind regression. If you understand this, you understand least squares."

Deriving the formula: where does the projection land?

The closest point on the line is \(\mathbf{p} = \lambda \mathbf{b}\) for some scalar \(\lambda\). Which \(\lambda\)?

Step 1. The residual must be perpendicular to the line:

\[\mathbf{b}^\top \underbrace{(\mathbf{x} - \lambda \mathbf{b})}_{\text{residual}} = 0\]

Step 2. Expand:

\[\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{x} - \lambda\,\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{b} = 0\]

Step 3. Solve for \(\lambda\):

\[\boxed{\lambda = \frac{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{b}}}\]
The projection formula is not magic — it comes directly from one requirement: make the error perpendicular.
"This is a three-line derivation that every student should be able to reproduce. Start from the perpendicularity condition, expand, solve for lambda. That's it. The formula is a consequence of one geometric principle: perpendicular = closest."

Projection: a concrete example

Line direction: \(\mathbf{b} = (1,1)\). Point: \(\mathbf{x} = (2,0)\).

\[\mathbf{p} = \frac{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{b}}\,\mathbf{b} = \frac{2}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\]

Closest point on the line to \((2,0)\) is \((1,1)\).

"Let's do the calculation step by step. The numerator b^T x is the dot product we learned in Unit 1 — it measures how much x agrees with b. The denominator b^T b is the squared length of b. We divide, then scale b by that ratio. The result (1,1) is the point on the line closest to (2,0)."

The residual: what's left over

The residual is the gap between the original point and the projection:

\[\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p} = (2,0) - (1,1) = (1,-1)\]
  • The residual points from the projection back to the original point.
  • Its length \(\norm{\mathbf{r}} = \sqrt{1^2+(-1)^2} = \sqrt{2}\) is the distance from \(\mathbf{x}\) to the line.
Key property: the residual is perpendicular to the line.

Check: \(\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{b} = (1)(1) + (-1)(1) = 0\). ✓ Perpendicular!

"The residual is the error — what the line can't explain. And it's perpendicular to the line, which is exactly the hill-to-road intuition: the shortest path is the straight-down path. This perpendicularity is what makes the projection optimal."

Why perpendicular = optimal

Think about it with the Pythagorean theorem:

\[\norm{\mathbf{x}}^2 = \norm{\mathbf{p}}^2 + \norm{\mathbf{r}}^2\]
  • The projection and residual form a right triangle.
  • If the error had any component along the line, you could slide closer.
  • Perpendicular means: nothing left to improve.
"This is just the Pythagorean theorem from high school. The vector x is the hypotenuse. The projection is one leg. The residual is the other leg. Because they form a right angle, the residual is as short as possible. If you moved the projection point left or right along the line, the residual would get longer."

Proof: any other point on the line is farther

Let \(\mathbf{p} = \lambda \mathbf{b}\) be the projection and \(\mathbf{q} = \mu \mathbf{b}\) be any other point on the line (\(\mu \neq \lambda\)).

Split the distance to \(\mathbf{q}\): \[\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{q} = \underbrace{(\mathbf{x} - \lambda\mathbf{b})}_{\mathbf{r}} + \underbrace{(\lambda - \mu)\mathbf{b}}_{\text{along the line}}\] Since \(\mathbf{r} \perp \mathbf{b}\), the Pythagorean theorem gives: \[\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{q}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{r}\|^2 + (\lambda-\mu)^2\|\mathbf{b}\|^2\]
  • \(\|\mathbf{r}\|^2\) is the distance to the projection — fixed.
  • \((\lambda-\mu)^2\|\mathbf{b}\|^2 > 0\) — moving away from \(\mathbf{p}\) only adds distance.
  • Therefore \(\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{q}\| > \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}\|\). The projection is uniquely closest.
"This is the proof. It uses one fact: perpendicular vectors satisfy the Pythagorean theorem. The distance to any other point on the line equals the distance to the projection PLUS an extra positive term. So the projection is strictly the closest. Three lines of algebra, one big idea."

Quick check

If \(\mathbf{x}\) already lies on the line through \(\mathbf{b}\), what is the projection of \(\mathbf{x}\)?

\(\mathbf{x}\) itself — it's already on the line, so the closest point is itself. The residual is zero.

In AI terms: if the model already perfectly predicts the data, the error is zero and there's nothing to improve.

The projection formula

Onto a line (1D subspace \(U = \text{span}\{\mathbf{b}\}\)): \[\pi_U(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{b}}\,\mathbf{b}\] Onto a subspace (with basis columns \(B\)): \[\pi_U(\mathbf{x}) = B(B^\top B)^{-1}B^\top \mathbf{x}\]
  • We proved the first one; we have not proved the second. It is the same three-step argument — residual ⊥ subspace, expand, solve — with the basis matrix \(B\) in place of the single vector \(\mathbf{b}\). We will do that derivation in L04, where it goes by another name: the normal equation.
  • Compare the two shapes: dividing by the number \(\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{b}\) becomes multiplying by the matrix inverse \((B^\top B)^{-1}\). Same formula, one dimension up.
  • The line formula uses the dot product from Unit 1.
  • The subspace formula uses matrix multiplication from Unit 1.
  • Three properties: (1) result lies in the subspace, (2) it's unique, (3) residual is perpendicular.
"Notice how everything builds on Unit 1. The dot product in the numerator measures agreement. The matrix multiplication in the general formula is the multi-dimensional version. Units in this course are like Lego blocks — each one snaps onto the previous."

The big connection: least squares = projection

Minimizing \(\norm{A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}}\) is the same as projecting \(\mathbf{b}\) onto the column space of \(A\).
  • \(A\mathbf{x}\) lives in the column space of \(A\) — the set of all outputs the system can produce.
  • The best-fit \(\mathbf{x}^*\) makes \(A\mathbf{x}^*\) the closest point in that column space to \(\mathbf{b}\).
  • The residual \(\mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x}^*\) is perpendicular to the column space.
This one idea — best fit is a projection — is the geometric foundation of all linear AI models.
"This is the single most important idea in this unit. When you train a linear model, you're projecting the true labels onto the space of things the model can produce. The best model is the closest point. And 'closest' means perpendicular residual. Everything else in this unit is just computing that projection."

Example: fitting a line through data

Three data points \((t_i, y_i)\): \((0, 1)\), \((1, 2)\), \((2, 2)\). The line is \(\hat{y} = w_0 + w_1 t\).

Fitting the line means finding \(w_0\) and \(w_1\). Fix those two numbers and the line is fixed — they are the unknowns.

Substitute each point and you get one equation per point:

\[\begin{aligned} w_0 + w_1(0) &= 1\\ w_0 + w_1(1) &= 2\\ w_0 + w_1(2) &= 2 \end{aligned}\]

Three equations, two unknowns. Now stack the coefficients — row \(i\) holds what multiplies \(w_0\) and \(w_1\) in equation \(i\):

\[\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} w_0 \\ w_1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\text{that is}\qquad A\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{b}\]
"Do not hand them the matrix and then justify it. Write the three equations first — substituting each point is something they can do without being told — and the matrix is then just those equations stacked." "The column of ones stops being mysterious the moment the equations are on the board: w0 is added unchanged to every prediction, so its coefficient is 1 in every row. Nobody put it there by hand; it is what the equations say." "Three equations, two unknowns: that is the overdetermined system from earlier, now with real data in it."

Reading that matrix

  • The column of \(1\)s is not extra data. It is \(w_0\)'s coefficient, and it is \(1\) in every equation because \(w_0\) is added to every prediction unchanged.
  • The second column is \(w_1\)'s coefficient — the measured \(t_i\).
  • \(\mathbf{b}\) is the observed \(y_i\). So \(A\mathbf{w}\) is what the model predicts, \(\mathbf{b}\) is what was observed.
  • Drop the \(1\) column and every equation becomes \(w_1 t_i = y_i\): a line forced through the origin.
No \(\mathbf{w}\) satisfies all three equations at once. That is the overdetermined system again — and the next slide says what to do about it.
"Ask what the model can produce: every vector of the form Aw, as w ranges over all pairs. That set is the column space, and it is a plane in R^3 while b is a point off it." "The origin point is worth making concrete — set w0 = 0 and the line through (0,1), (1,2), (2,2) has to start at (0,0), which is visibly wrong."

Fitting a line is a projection

  • Careful — \(\mathbf{b}\) has changed jobs. Before, \(\mathbf{b}\) was the direction of the line we projected onto. Here it is the vector being projected.
  • The subspace is the column space of \(A\) — every vector \(A\mathbf{w}\) the model can possibly produce, a plane inside \(\R^3\).
  • We want the point on that plane closest to \(\mathbf{b}\): a projection, exactly what we just built.
One more rename, then we are settled. From L04 on the same picture gets its regression names: \(A \to X\) (data matrix), \(\mathbf{b} \to \mathbf{y}\) (observed), \(\mathbf{x} \to \mathbf{w}\) (weights), \(A\mathbf{x} \to \hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\mathbf{w}\) (predicted). Same geometry, different alphabet.
"This is the payoff of L03: fitting a line is not a new technique, it is the projection they just derived, with the subspace being everything the model can produce." "The rename matters because L04 onward uses X and y throughout, and a student who does not carry the correspondence will think a new method has appeared."

Quick check

If all three points lie exactly on a line, what is the least-squares residual?

Zero — \(\mathbf{b}\) is already in the column space of \(A\), so the "closest point" is \(\mathbf{b}\) itself.

L03 takeaways

  • Overdetermined systems have more equations than unknowns — no exact answer. [MASTER]
  • Projection gives the closest point; the residual is perpendicular. [MASTER]
  • Projection formula onto a line: \(\lambda = \frac{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{b}}\). [MASTER]
  • Subspace projection formula: \(B(B^\top B)^{-1}B^\top\). [UNDERSTAND]
Next lecture: we'll use this geometry to derive a formula for the best-fit line. That formula is how every linear regression model works.

L04 — Predicting apartment rent

You're apartment hunting. You notice two things: bigger apartments cost more, and being farther from campus costs less.

ApartmentSize Distance to campusRent
A600 sqft1 mile$3,000
B800 sqft2 miles$2,000
C1000 sqft3 miles$4,000
Can we find a formula that predicts rent from size and distance? And once we can write one down — which formula is best?
"Only three apartments so every number stays on the board. Note B: bigger than A but cheaper, because it is farther out. That is the whole reason we need two coefficients rather than one."

Your data is a matrix

What are we actually trying to do? Take one apartment's numbers, multiply each by a coefficient, add them up, and get a predicted rent:

\[\text{predicted rent} = w_0\cdot(\text{size}) \;+\; w_1\cdot(\text{distance})\]

\(w_0\) is "dollars per 100 sqft" and \(w_1\) is "dollars per mile." Those two numbers are the model. Find them and you can price any apartment; the rest of this lecture is about how to find the best pair.

Stack the inputs into \(X\) and the thing we want to predict into \(\mathbf{y}\):

\[\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 6 & 1 \\ 8 & 2 \\ 10 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{X:\ \text{size (100s sqft)},\ \text{distance (miles)}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{y}:\ \text{rent (\$1000s)}}\]

Each row of \(X\) is one apartment; each column is one feature. \(\mathbf{y}\) is not part of \(X\) — it is the answer we are trying to reproduce.

"Say the sentence before showing the matrix. Students meet 'weights' two slides later and have no idea what a weight is unless they have already been told we are looking for two multipliers. Units help: dollars per 100 sqft, dollars per mile."

Prediction = matrix × weights

We do not know the best \(w_0, w_1\) yet — so guess a pair and see what happens. Try \(\mathbf{w} = (0.4,\,-0.6)^\top\): each 100 sqft adds \$400, each mile subtracts \$600.

\[\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 1 \\ 8 & 2 \\ 10 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0.4 \\ -0.6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6(0.4)+1(-0.6) \\ 8(0.4)+2(-0.6) \\ 10(0.4)+3(-0.6) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.8 \\ 2.0 \\ 2.2 \end{bmatrix}\]

Each prediction is a dot product: \(\hat{y}_i = \mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{w}\), where \(\mathbf{x}_i\) is apartment \(i\)'s row.

Actual rents are \((3,\,2,\,4)\). This guess predicts everything at roughly \$2000 — it barely distinguishes the three apartments at all.

"Emphasise that 0.4 and -0.6 were picked out of the air. The point of guessing is to have something to measure, so that 'best' means something by the end of the lecture."

Mean Squared Error: one number for "how bad"

True rents \(\mathbf{y} = (3,\,2,\,4)\) against the guess \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = (1.8,\,2.0,\,2.2)\):

ApartmentTrue PredictedError Squared error
A3.01.81.21.44
B2.02.00.00.00
C4.02.21.83.24
\[\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2 = \frac{1.44+0.00+3.24}{3} = \mathbf{1.56}\]

One number for a whole model. Lower is better — so "find the best \(\mathbf{w}\)" now means something precise: make this number as small as it can go.

"Apartment B is predicted exactly right and still the model is bad. Worth saying: MSE judges the model as a whole, not one lucky row."

MSE in matrix notation

\[J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{n}\norm{X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{w})^2\] \[\underbrace{X}_{n\times d} \qquad \underbrace{\mathbf{w}}_{d\times 1} \qquad \underbrace{\mathbf{y}}_{n\times 1} \qquad \underbrace{X\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}}_{n\times 1} \qquad \underbrace{J(\mathbf{w})}_{1\times 1}\]
  • \(n\) = examples (rows), \(d\) = features (columns). One row per apartment, one weight per feature — so \(X\mathbf{w}\) is only defined because \(X\)'s columns match \(\mathbf{w}\)'s entries.
  • \(X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\) is the residual vector from L03 — still \(n\) numbers, one error per apartment. The norm is what collapses those \(n\) numbers into the single number \(J\) we can then minimize.
  • \(\norm{\cdot}^2\) is the squared \(\ell_2\) norm from Unit 1 — squaring cancels the square root inside it, which is why the left side is a plain sum of squares on the right.
  • Everything connects: dot product → norm → residual → MSE.
The entire vocabulary of Unit 1 (vectors, norms, dot products) was preparation for this moment: measuring model error.
"See how everything fits together? The dot product computes each prediction. The norm measures the total error. The residual is the gap between prediction and truth. MSE is just the squared norm of the residual, divided by n. This is not a coincidence — we designed the course this way."

Quick check

If one residual doubles (say apartment B's error goes from 0.8 to 1.6), how does its squared contribution change?

It quadruples — from 0.64 to 2.56. Squared error amplifies large mistakes.

This is why AI models care more about big errors than small ones. A prediction that's off by 10 counts 100× as much as one that's off by 1.

Wait — we've seen this before!

Minimize \(\norm{X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}}\) — this is exactly the least squares problem from L03!
  • \(X\mathbf{w}^*\) is the projection of \(\mathbf{y}\) onto the column space of \(X\).
  • The residual \(\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}^*\) is perpendicular to the column space.
  • The best weights \(\mathbf{w}^*\) are the coordinates of that projection.
The best-fit line isn't arbitrary — it's the unique prediction that is geometrically closest to the true data.
"This is the payoff from L03. We learned that the closest point is a projection, and the residual is perpendicular. Now we're applying the same idea: the best linear model is the one whose predictions are the projection of the true labels onto the model's output space."

Deriving the normal equation

We know from L03: the best fit makes the residual perpendicular to the column space. Let's turn this into a formula.

Step 1. The residual \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}\) must be perpendicular to every column of \(X\).

Step 2. Say that in symbols — each row of \(X^\top\) dots with \(\mathbf{r}\) and gives zero:

\[X^\top \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{0}\]

Step 3. Substitute \(\mathbf{r}\):

\[X^\top(\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{0}\]

Step 4. Expand and rearrange:

\[X^\top \mathbf{y} - X^\top X \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{X^\top X \,\mathbf{w} = X^\top \mathbf{y}}\]
Four steps: goal → math → substitute → rearrange. The normal equation is a direct consequence of perpendicularity.
"This is the same three-line derivation as the projection formula from L03, just in matrix form. Step 1: state the geometric principle (perpendicular). Step 2: write it as an equation. Step 3-4: do the algebra. I want every student to be able to reproduce this on the board."

The normal equation: summary

\[X^\top X \,\mathbf{w} = X^\top \mathbf{y}\]
  • What it does: it hands you the \(\mathbf{w}\) that minimizes the MSE we wrote down earlier — \[\mathbf{w}^* = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w}} J(\mathbf{w}), \qquad J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{n}\norm{X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}}^2\] Solving one linear system replaces searching over every possible \(\mathbf{w}\).
  • Where it comes from: residual ⊥ column space (the projection principle).
  • If \(X^\top X\) is invertible: \(\mathbf{w}^* = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top \mathbf{y}\).
  • The word "normal" means perpendicular (not "ordinary").
  • Promise from L03, now paid. Substitute \(\mathbf{w}^*\) back to get the prediction: \[\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\mathbf{w}^* = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top \mathbf{y}\] That is the subspace projection formula \(\pi_U(\mathbf{x}) = B(B^\top B)^{-1}B^\top\mathbf{x}\) we stated without proof, with \(B = X\) — and the 1D version \(\lambda = \frac{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{b}}\) is the same statement with a single column. One geometric principle, three costumes.
"Compare: in 1D, b^T(x - λb) = 0 gave us λ = b^T x / b^T b. In multiple dimensions, X^T(y - Xw) = 0 gives us X^T X w = X^T y. Same idea, bigger matrices."

Worked example: solve it by hand (1 of 2)

Same three apartments. Now stop guessing and solve \(X^\top X\,\mathbf{w} = X^\top\mathbf{y}\):

\[X = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 1 \\ 8 & 2 \\ 10 & 3 \end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix}3\\2\\4\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix}w_0\\w_1\end{bmatrix}\]

Every entry of \(X^\top X\) is a dot product of two columns of \(X\):

\[X^\top X = \begin{bmatrix} 6^2+8^2+10^2 & 6(1)+8(2)+10(3)\\[2pt] 6(1)+8(2)+10(3) & 1^2+2^2+3^2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}200 & 52\\ 52 & 14\end{bmatrix}\]
\[X^\top\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 6(3)+8(2)+10(4)\\[2pt] 1(3)+2(2)+3(4)\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}74\\ 19\end{bmatrix}\]

So the whole problem is now \(\begin{bmatrix}200 & 52\\ 52 & 14\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{w}=\begin{bmatrix}74\\19\end{bmatrix}\) — two equations, two unknowns.

"Have them compute one entry out loud. X-transpose-X is symmetric because the off-diagonal is the same dot product read twice — worth pointing at."

Worked example: solve it by hand (2 of 2)

Step 1 — is it even invertible? \(\det = 200(14) - 52^2 = 2800 - 2704 = \mathbf{96}\).

Not zero, so \((X^\top X)^{-1}\) exists. Notice you had to actually subtract to find that out — \(2800\) and \(2704\) are not far apart. Hold on to that thought; L06 is about what happens when they are closer still.

Step 2 — invert and multiply, with the \(2\times2\) formula you know:

\[\mathbf{w}^* = \frac{1}{96}\begin{bmatrix}14 & -52\\ -52 & 200\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}74\\19\end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{96}\begin{bmatrix}1036-988\\ -3848+3800\end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{96}\begin{bmatrix}48\\ -48\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}\ \ 0.5\\ -0.5\end{bmatrix}\]

Step 3 — check it. Predictions \(X\mathbf{w}^* = (2.5,\,3,\,3.5)\), residual \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{y}-X\mathbf{w}^* = (0.5,\,-1,\,0.5)\), and

\[X^\top\mathbf{r} = \begin{bmatrix}6(0.5)+8(-1)+10(0.5)\\ 1(0.5)+2(-1)+3(0.5)\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}\ \checkmark\]

The residual really is perpendicular to both columns — exactly what we demanded.

\(\mathbf{w}^*=(0.5,-0.5)\): each 100 sqft adds \$500, each mile costs \$500. MSE drops from 1.56 to 0.5, and no searching was needed — one determinant, one \(2\times2\) inverse. This is what LinearRegression does inside.
"Three steps, and step 1 is the one students skip: check the determinant before inverting. It costs one subtraction and it is the difference between an answer and nonsense — which is exactly the lecture after this one."

Quick check

If \(X^\top X\) is not invertible, what does that suggest about the columns of \(X\)?

The columns are linearly dependent — some features are redundant. For example, if one column is twice another, you can't tell which feature is "doing the work."

Real-world example: if you include both temperature in °C and °F as features, one is redundant. The formula can't separate their contributions.

L04 takeaways

  • Regression predicts with \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\mathbf{w}\) — matrix × vector. [MASTER]
  • MSE is the one number that defines "best fit." [MASTER]
  • The normal equation \(X^\top X\mathbf{w} = X^\top \mathbf{y}\) — a direct formula. [MASTER]
  • Solve by hand for small examples (1-2 parameters). [MASTER]
But the normal equation has limits: it requires computing \(X^\top X\) and inverting it. What if that matrix is too big, or nearly singular? Next lectures: we'll explore when things go wrong — and what to do about it.

L05 — Can you take all the classes you want?

You want to take 4 classes, but some meet at the same time. Can you find a schedule that satisfies every constraint?

  • Each constraint: "this class needs this time slot."
  • Sometimes all constraints can be satisfied (exact solution).
  • Sometimes some conflict — you have to compromise.
Linear systems are the math of constraints. Today: when do exact solutions exist, and what structure tells us?
"Everyone's experienced this: you want certain classes, but time slots conflict. A linear system is the mathematical version of this problem. Each equation is a constraint. We want to know: can all constraints be satisfied simultaneously? If not, what's the best we can do? We already know the 'best we can do' part from L03–L04. Today we focus on when exact solutions exist."

Example: two equations, two unknowns

You and a friend eat at the same taco place. Two receipts:

  • You: 1 taco + 1 drink = $5
  • Friend: 2 tacos + 1 drink = $8

Let \(x\) = price of a taco, \(y\) = price of a drink. Each receipt is one equation:

\[\begin{cases} x + y = 5 \\ 2x + y = 8 \end{cases}\]

Subtract the first from the second: \(x = 3\). Put that back: \(y = 2\).

Taco $3, drink $2. One exact solution.

"Both lines are things you could actually be handed at a counter. Subtracting one receipt from the other cancels the drink and leaves one taco — that is elimination, and they have just done it without being told the name."

Three possible outcomes for a linear system

A linear system \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) can have:
  1. Exactly one solution — lines cross at one point.
  2. No solution — parallel lines, contradictory constraints.
  3. Infinitely many — same line, redundant constraints.
"In 2D, these correspond to lines crossing, lines being parallel, or lines being the same. In higher dimensions, the geometry is harder to visualize but the three cases still apply."

Quick check

A third friend orders exactly what you ordered — 1 taco + 1 drink — and the register charges $10.
\(\begin{cases} x + y = 5 \\ x + y = 10 \end{cases}\)

No solution. \(x + y\) cannot be both 5 and 10. The same order rang up two different totals, so the two receipts contradict each other — parallel lines, no intersection.

"This one is worth a beat: it is not that the maths failed. The data is inconsistent, and the geometry is telling you so."

Column space: what can the model produce?

Think of a vending machine with two buttons. Button A dispenses a combo of items, Button B dispenses a different combo. You can press each button any number of times.

  • The column space is everything you can get from combining button presses.
  • If what you want is in the column space → exact solution exists.
  • If it's outside the column space → no exact solution.
The column space of \(A\): \(\text{col}(A) = \{A\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n\}\) — all possible outputs.

\(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) has a solution \(\iff\) \(\mathbf{b} \in \text{col}(A)\).
"The column space is all the targets the system can hit. Think of it as the menu of the vending machine. If what you want is on the menu, great. If not, the best you can do is get the closest available item — and that's the projection from L03."

Example: what can this matrix produce?

\[A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\] Any \(A\mathbf{x}\) has the form \(\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\0\end{pmatrix}\).
  • The column space is a plane in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) — the \(xy\)-plane.
  • Can we hit \(\mathbf{b} = (1, 2, 3)^\top\)? No — the third entry must be 0.
  • Can we hit \(\mathbf{b} = (1, 2, 0)^\top\)? Yes — \(\mathbf{x} = (1, 2)^\top\).
In AI: the column space tells you what your model can predict. A linear model can't produce curved predictions — that's outside its column space.
"This matrix can only produce vectors with a zero in the third position. It's like a model that can't predict certain patterns — they're outside its capabilities. When the target is outside the column space, we project — which is exactly regression."

Rank: how many independent directions?

The rank of \(A\) = dimension of its column space = number of independent columns.
  • Rank 1: the model can only produce multiples of one direction.
  • Rank 2: the model spans a plane.
  • Rank = number of independent columns.
More independent features = higher rank = more flexible model. But too much flexibility leads to overfitting — a topic for Unit 5.
"Rank is like the degrees of freedom of your model. A rank-1 model can only scale one pattern. A rank-2 model can mix two patterns. Ideally you have as many independent patterns as you need to explain the data — but not more."

Quick check

If every row of \(A\) has last entry zero, what must be true about \(\mathbf{b}\) for \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) to be solvable?

The last entry of \(\mathbf{b}\) must be zero — because \(A\mathbf{x}\) can never produce anything else in that position.

Putting it together: writing it as \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\)

Back to the very first problem of this unit. Three friends guess your age: \(19,\;21,\;20\).

What is unknown? One thing — your age, \(x\). One unknown quantity, so \(x\) is a single number, not a vector.

Each guess is a claim about that number, so each guess is one equation:

\[1\cdot x = 19,\qquad 1\cdot x = 21,\qquad 1\cdot x = 20\]

The coefficient is \(1\) every time: each friend is guessing \(x\) itself, unscaled.

Stack the coefficients into \(A\), the guesses into \(\mathbf{b}\):

\[\underbrace{A = \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}}_{3\times 1},\qquad \underbrace{\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix}19\\21\\20\end{bmatrix}}_{3\times 1}, \qquad A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\]

\(A\) is \(3\times1\), so \(\mathbf{x}\) is \(1\times1\) — the shapes force what the story already said. Three equations, one unknown: overdetermined.

"Why x is one number: there is one unknown thing in the world, your age. Then the shapes are forced. Keep A x = b — this is a linear system, not a prediction."

Is the target even reachable?

Recall: \(\mathrm{col}(A)\) is every vector \(A\mathbf{x}\) the system can produce — everything reachable, and nothing else.

For our \(A\), one column and one unknown:

\[A\mathbf{x} = x\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}x\\x\\x\end{bmatrix}\]

\(\mathrm{col}(A)\) is every vector whose three entries are equal — a line through the origin in \(\R^3\) along \((1,1,1)\).

Is \(\mathbf{b} = (19,21,20)\) of that form? It would need \(19 = 21 = 20\). It is not.
So \(\mathbf{b}\notin\mathrm{col}(A)\): no \(x\) satisfies all three equations. That is what "no exact solution" means here — not a rule, just three different numbers.
"Membership in the column space is checkable by eye for this A. b fails the check, so the system is inconsistent — and that is the whole reason we are about to project."

So compute the projection

\(\mathbf{b}\) is not reachable, so take the closest point that is: project \(\mathbf{b}\) onto \(\mathrm{col}(A)\), using the L03 formula with \(A\) as the direction:

\[A^\top A = 1+1+1 = 3,\qquad A^\top\mathbf{b} = 19+21+20 = 60\] \[x^* = \frac{A^\top\mathbf{b}}{A^\top A} = \frac{60}{3} = \mathbf{20} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad A x^* = \begin{bmatrix}20\\20\\20\end{bmatrix}\]

Residual, and the perpendicularity check:

\[\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - Ax^* = \begin{bmatrix}-1\\ 1\\ 0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad A^\top\mathbf{r} = -1+1+0 = 0\ \checkmark\]

So the closest reachable point is \((20,20,20)\), and the best single answer is \(x^* = 20\) — the average of 19, 21 and 20.

"Compute it in front of them: three additions and one division. Let the last line sit for a second before turning the page — most of the room will notice it is the average on their own."

Wait — is that a coincidence?

Back in L03 we proved a theorem: for any numbers \(b_1,\dots,b_n\), the total squared miss \(\sum_i (x-b_i)^2\) is smallest at exactly the average. We got it by differentiating, and for these three friends it gave \(3x = 60\), so \(x=20\).

We have just arrived at \(20\) again by a completely different route — column space and orthogonal projection, no calculus anywhere.

Two arguments with nothing in common, same answer. Is that luck? Take a moment before the next slide.
"Stop and actually ask. Some will say coincidence, some will say it must mean something. The answer lands harder if they have committed to a guess."

No — the average is the normal equation, for this one \(A\)

Put our \(A = (1,1,1)^\top\) into the general formula and watch what each piece becomes:

\[A^\top A = \underbrace{1+1+\cdots+1}_{n} = n, \qquad A^\top\mathbf{b} = b_1+\cdots+b_n = \sum_i b_i\]

So \(A^\top A\,x = A^\top\mathbf{b}\) reads

\[n\,x = \sum_i b_i \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i b_i = \bar b\]

That is the L03 theorem — the same equation the derivative gave us there.

Not a coincidence: a special case. The L03 theorem is what the normal equation becomes when \(A\) is a single column of \(1\)s. Averaging was never a separate technique — it is least squares with one all-ones feature.

The perpendicularity condition specialises too: \(A^\top\mathbf{r}=0\) becomes \(\sum_i r_i = 0\) — the deviations from the mean sum to zero, which you have known since school without knowing it was an orthogonality statement.

"They have known since school that deviations from the mean cancel. That fact is A-transpose-r = 0. The general machinery did not replace what they knew — it explained it."

That check you just did has a name — you already met it

You verified \(A^\top\mathbf{r} = 0\) as a sanity check. That is not a coincidence. Substitute \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x}\):

\[A^\top(\mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{0} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad A^\top A\,\mathbf{x} = A^\top\mathbf{b}\]

In L04 we derived the same equation, written in the regression names: \(X^\top X\mathbf{w} = X^\top\mathbf{y}\). Same object, two vocabularies:

solving a system \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) \(A\) = coefficients, \(\mathbf{x}\) = unknowns, \(\mathbf{b}\) = targets
fitting a model \(X\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{y}\) \(X\) = data, \(\mathbf{w}\) = weights, \(\mathbf{y}\) = observations
We came back to it because column space is what L04 was missing. There we could write \(A^\top\mathbf{r} = 0\) but not say what \(\mathbf{r}\) was perpendicular to. Now we can: to everything the system can produce. The residual is the part of \(\mathbf{b}\) no choice of \(\mathbf{x}\) can reach.
"Say out loud that this is a return, not a new topic, and put the two notations side by side once — students hit both in the book and in every course after this one."

Quick check

If \(\mathbf{y}\) is already in the column space of \(X\), what is the residual at the normal-equation solution?

Zero — \(\mathbf{y}\) is already on the plane, so no perpendicular drop is needed.

L05 takeaways

  • Linear systems: three outcomes (one, none, infinitely many solutions). [MASTER]
  • Column space determines which targets are reachable. [UNDERSTAND]
  • Rank = number of independent columns = flexibility of the model. [UNDERSTAND]

L06 — When the formula gives garbage

Two systems. Look at how little separates them, then look at the answers.

\[\begin{cases} x + y = 2\\[2pt] x + \mathbf{0.999}\,y = 1.998 \end{cases}\]

solve \(\Rightarrow\) \(\;(x,y) = (0,\;2)\)

\[\begin{cases} x + y = 2\\[2pt] x + \mathbf{1.001}\,y = 1.998 \end{cases}\]

solve \(\Rightarrow\) \(\;(x,y) = (4,\;-2)\)

One coefficient moved by \(0.002\). Check both — they are exact:

\[0 + 0.999(2) = 1.998 \qquad\qquad 4 + 1.001(-2) = 4 - 2.002 = 1.998\]
The input changed in the third decimal place. \(x\) went from \(0\) to \(4\), and \(y\) changed sign. Nothing was solved incorrectly — both answers are right for the system above them. So what went wrong?
"Put the two systems up and say nothing for a few seconds. Someone will spot that the only difference is 0.999 versus 1.001. Then reveal the answers. No figure on purpose: an ill-conditioned system looks completely ordinary, and that is exactly the problem."

Two different things can go wrong

Our formula is \(\mathbf{w} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}\). It can fail in two separate ways:

  1. Does that inverse even exist? \(X\) is tall, not square, so \(X^{-1}\) does not exist at all — and \(X^\top X\) can fail to be invertible too.
  2. If it exists, can I trust the number it gives? That is what you just watched fail.
Separate problems, separate answers. Existence first — there is no point asking whether to trust a number you cannot compute. Then trust.
"This slide is the map for the rest of the lecture. Point back at it when you switch from Worry 1 to Worry 2."

Worry 1: does the formula even exist?

We can only invert square matrices. But data matrices are usually tall (more rows than columns):

\[X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{(3 data points, 1 parameter)}\] \(X\) is 3×1 — no inverse exists. But we still want to "undo" \(X\) to find the best weights.
For a matrix \(A\) with linearly independent columns, define \[A^+ = (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top,\qquad \mathbf{x}^*=A^+\mathbf{b}.\]

MML §2.3.4, pp. 34–35, eq. (2.59). The independent-columns condition is what makes \(A^\top A\) invertible.

"Motivation first: a tall data matrix has no ordinary inverse. Now the definition. Read it from right to left: transpose the target, then solve with A-transpose-A. MML states both the independent-columns condition and the numerical warning on these pages."

Why call \(A^+\) an inverse?

First check: if \(A\) is square and invertible, the new formula gives the old inverse:

\[ A^+=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top =A^{-1}(A^\top)^{-1}A^\top =A^{-1}. \]

One identity drives everything else:

\[ (A^\top A)A^+ =(A^\top A)(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top =A^\top. \]
So \(A^+\) really extends the inverse we already know; it is not just a new label for the normal-equation answer.
"The first line checks the name against a case we trust. The second line is the engine for the two claims on the next slide. Nothing is being asserted from analogy; both facts drop out of the definition."

What does \(A^+\mathbf{b}\) return?

Exact case. If \(\mathbf{b}=A\mathbf{x}_0\), then

\[ A^+\mathbf{b}=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top A\mathbf{x}_0 =\mathbf{x}_0. \]

No exact solution. Let \(\mathbf{x}^*=A^+\mathbf{b}\). The identity from the last slide gives

\[ A^\top A\mathbf{x}^*=A^\top\mathbf{b} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A^\top(\mathbf{b}-A\mathbf{x}^*)=\mathbf{0}. \]
So the residual is perpendicular to every column of \(A\): \(\mathbf{x}^*\) is exactly the least-squares solution from L03–L04.
"State, then prove. In the exact case the A-transpose-A cancels. In the inexact case the same identity lands on the normal equation, so the residual is perpendicular to the column space. That is why this formula returns the closest reachable output."

Example: build \(X^+\), then multiply

Return to the same three apartments from L04:

\[ X=\begin{bmatrix}6&1\\8&2\\10&3\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\\4\end{bmatrix}. \]

L04 already found \((X^\top X)^{-1}=\frac1{96}\begin{bmatrix}14&-52\\-52&200\end{bmatrix}\). Now actually form the pseudoinverse:

\[ X^+=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top =\frac1{96}\begin{bmatrix}32&8&-16\\-112&-16&80\end{bmatrix}. \]

Then use it:

\[ \mathbf{w}^*=X^+\mathbf{y} =\frac1{96}\begin{bmatrix}48\\-48\end{bmatrix} =\boxed{\begin{bmatrix}0.5\\-0.5\end{bmatrix}}. \]
Same data, same answer as L04 — but now the pseudoinverse was constructed before it was used.
"This is not the normal equation with a pseudoinverse label pasted on afterward. We build X-plus as a two-by-three matrix and then multiply it by y. The result must match L04 because the proof on the previous slide says it solves the same least-squares problem."

Quick check

If \(\mathbf{b}\) lies in the column space of \(A\), what does \(A^+\mathbf{b}\) give?

The exact solution — no approximation needed. With the independent columns assumed here, that solution is unique.

Worry 2: it exists — can we trust it?

The pseudoinverse settled existence. Now back to the two systems we opened with.

Write them as matrices. Same right-hand side both times — only one coefficient differs:

\[A_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & \mathbf{0.999} \end{bmatrix},\qquad A_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & \mathbf{1.001} \end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\1.998\end{bmatrix}\]

You already know the \(2\times2\) inverse: \(\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d\end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a\end{bmatrix}\). Everything depends on that \(ad-bc\), so compute it first:

\[\det A_1 = 0.999 - 1 = \mathbf{-0.001}, \qquad \det A_2 = 1.001 - 1 = \mathbf{+0.001}\]
The coefficient moved by \(0.002\) — and the determinant crossed zero. So \(1/\det\) went from \(-1000\) to \(+1000\). Whatever the inverse looks like, it is about to be a thousand times the size of the matrix itself.
"Do not rush past the sign. The determinant did not just get smaller, it went from negative to positive, which means somewhere between 0.999 and 1.001 there is a matrix with no inverse at all. The two systems in the opening sit on either side of that cliff."

Watch the nudge travel through

Build both inverses with the formula. The \(1/\det\) out front is \(\mp 1000\):

\[A_1^{-1} = \frac{1}{-0.001}\begin{bmatrix}0.999 & -1\\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}-999 & 1000\\ 1000 & -1000\end{bmatrix}, \qquad A_2^{-1} = \frac{1}{+0.001}\begin{bmatrix}1.001 & -1\\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1001 & -1000\\ -1000 & 1000\end{bmatrix}\]

Every entry of \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) is about 1. Every entry of their inverses is about 1000, and the two have opposite signs.

Now multiply each by the same \(\mathbf{b} = (2,\,1.998)\):

\[A_1^{-1}\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix}-1998+1998\\ 2000-1998\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\2\end{bmatrix}, \qquad A_2^{-1}\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix}2002-1998\\ -2000+1998\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4\\-2\end{bmatrix}\]
Those are the two answers from the opening slide, and now you can see where they came from. The coefficient moved by \(0.002\); \(1/\det\) swung from \(-1000\) to \(+1000\); the answer moved by \(4\). Nothing was computed incorrectly — the amplification is built into the formula.
"This closes the loop the lecture opened with. They saw the two answers on slide one and were told nothing was solved incorrectly. Here is the mechanism: same b, same arithmetic, and a determinant that nearly vanished between the two matrices."

So why do we look at \(X^\top X\)?

With real data \(X\) is tall — there is no \(X^{-1}\). Least squares inverts \(X^\top X\) instead: \(\;\mathbf{x}^* = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{b}\).

\[X^\top X = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 2.001\\ 2.001 & 2.002001\end{bmatrix}\] \[\det(X^\top X) = (2)(2.002001) - (2.001)^2 = 4.004002 - 4.004001 = \mathbf{0.000001}\]

That is no accident: \(\det(X^\top X) = (\det X)^2 = (0.001)^2 = 10^{-6}\).

Squaring the matrix squares the smallness of the determinant. \(0.001 \to 10^{-6}\): the thousand becomes a million.

The formula is correct. It is unreliable — and now you can see exactly where the unreliability enters.

"This answers the question a sharp student asks: why are we suddenly looking at X-transpose-X? Because that is the matrix least squares actually has to invert, and it carries the square of the problem."

Real data does this to you: °C and °F

Predict something from temperature, and you record it twice — once in Celsius, once in Fahrenheit:

\[X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 12 & 53.6\\ 1 & 18 & 64.4\\ 1 & 25 & 77.0\\ 1 & 31 & 87.8\end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix}2.1\\2.6\\3.4\\3.9\end{bmatrix}\]

Because \(F = 1.8\,C + 32\), the third column is built from the other two:

\[\text{col}_3 = 32\cdot\text{col}_1 + 1.8\cdot\text{col}_2\]

Check the first row: \(32(1) + 1.8(12) = 32 + 21.6 = 53.6\). ✓   It holds for every row.

The third feature carries no new information. \(\det(X^\top X) \approx 10^{-11}\) — singular for all practical purposes.

"Nobody sets out to add a useless column. You add temperature, someone else adds it in the other unit, and now your matrix is singular and nothing warns you."

What that costs you — run it and see

Solve the normal equation as written:

\[\mathbf{w} = (0.913,\; 0.097,\; 0.000)\]

Now change one Fahrenheit reading by 0.1 of a degree — less than the width of a thermometer mark — and solve again:

\[\mathbf{w} = (-22.106,\; -1.189,\; +0.717)\]

The Celsius coefficient went from \(+0.097\) to \(-1.189\). It changed sign.

But look at what the two models actually predict:

\[\begin{array}{lcccc} \text{before} & 2.08 & 2.66 & 3.34 & 3.92\\ \text{after} & 2.10 & 2.63 & 3.33 & 3.94\\ \hline \mathbf{y} & 2.1 & 2.6 & 3.4 & 3.9 \end{array}\]
Both fit the data equally well. The predictions are pinned down; the individual weights are not. Never read a coefficient from a model whose columns are nearly dependent.
"This is the punchline of the whole lecture. The model is fine. The number you wanted to interpret is not."

This problem has a name

What we measured with \(\det\) has a standard measure called the condition number \(\kappa(A)\): how much a small change in the data can be amplified in the answer.

MML defines it in §7.1, p.230:

\[\kappa(A) = \frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)}\]

— a ratio of singular values, which we meet in L07–L08. So the definition is genuinely ahead of us today.

Everything we needed this lecture, we got from \(\det\) and the \(2\times2\) inverse. The name is here so you recognise it when it returns in Week 8, where MML puts it.

"Say plainly: this is a real concept, it is in your book, and its definition needs machinery we do not have yet. We are not skipping it, we are sequencing it. In Python np.linalg.cond(A) computes it, and after L08 you will know what it is computing."

Three fixes — and what each one actually changes

The disease is always the same: \(\det(X^\top X)\) sits too close to zero, so \((X^\top X)^{-1}\) has enormous entries. Each fix attacks that directly.

  1. Drop one of the dependent columns. Delete °F and keep °C. You lose nothing — the column carried no information — and the determinant is no longer near zero.
  2. Ridge: solve \((X^\top X + \lambda I)\mathbf{w} = X^\top\mathbf{y}\). Adding \(\lambda\) down the diagonal pushes the determinant away from zero.
  3. Never form \(X^\top X\) at all (QR or SVD, what np.linalg.lstsq uses). If you do not square the matrix, you do not square the problem.
"Three fixes, one target. Ask the class which one they would reach for with the temperature data — dropping the column is obviously right there, and that is the point: the fix depends on why the columns are dependent."

Why ridge works — watch the determinant

Our \(X^\top X = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 2.001\\ 2.001 & 2.002001\end{bmatrix}\), with \(\det = 10^{-6}\). Add \(\lambda\) down the diagonal and recompute:

Ten times more \(\lambda\), ten times smaller inverse. That is the entire mechanism — and the cost is that you are no longer solving the original problem. Choosing \(\lambda\) is the trade, and it comes back in L19.
"Do the lambda = 0.001 row with them. The diagonal goes from 2 and 2.002001 to 2.001 and 2.003001, and the determinant jumps four-thousand-fold. Nothing subtle is happening — it is the 2x2 determinant they already know."

Quick check — and its proof

Two columns of \(X\) are exactly identical — the same feature entered twice. What happens to \(X^\top X\), and why?

It is singular. Here is the whole argument, in two lines.

If columns \(i\) and \(j\) are equal, take \(\mathbf{v}\) with \(+1\) in slot \(i\), \(-1\) in slot \(j\), zeros elsewhere. Then \(X\mathbf{v}\) is (column \(i\)) minus (column \(j\)) \(= \mathbf{0}\).

\[X^\top X\mathbf{v} = X^\top(X\mathbf{v}) = X^\top\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}\]

So a non-zero \(\mathbf{v}\) is killed by \(X^\top X\). A matrix with something non-zero in its null space cannot be inverted.

Nearly identical columns are the same argument with \(\approx\) in place of \(=\): \(X\mathbf{v}\) is not zero but is tiny, so \(\det\) is not zero but is tiny — which is exactly the case we spent this lecture computing.

"This is the cleanest proof in the lecture and it takes one minute. Ask them to produce v for a concrete 3x2 matrix before showing it."
