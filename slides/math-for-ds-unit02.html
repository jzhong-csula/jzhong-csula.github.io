L03 — Projection: The Geometry of "Closest"

Three friends guess your age:

AliceBobCarol
192120
  • Written as equations: \(x = 19\), \(x = 21\), \(x = 20\). No single number satisfies all three.
  • You cannot be right for everyone. You can only be wrong by as little as possible.
  • You'd probably say the average: \(x^* = \frac{19+21+20}{3} = 20\) — the best compromise.
But why the average? When the answers conflict, what does "best" mean — and how do we find it?
"Start with the simplest possible version: one unknown, three conflicting answers. Everyone's intuition says take the average. The intuition is right — the question is WHY. Answering that gives us a tool that scales all the way to training a neural network." Ask the class for their guess before revealing the average.

Why the average? Minimizing squared error

We never find out your real age. All we have is the three answers — so "error" here does not mean distance from the truth. It means how far our one answer sits from each friend's.

Pick a candidate \(x\). It misses Alice by \(x-19\), Bob by \(x-21\), Carol by \(x-20\). Square each one, so being 2 too high counts the same as being 2 too low. Then add them up:

The average gives the smallest total. Not a coincidence — it's a theorem, and it needed no true answer to find.
"Watch for this question, because it is the right one: how can we talk about error when we never learn the real age? We can't — and we don't have to. The quantity we minimize is disagreement with the data we were given, not distance from a truth we never see. That is exactly the situation in every AI model: you have training data, you never have the true function." "Why square? Two reasons: it stops a +2 and a -2 from cancelling to zero, and it makes a miss of 3 count nine times as much as a miss of 1." "The x here is not a fourth guess — it is our single proposed answer, the one number we have to commit to."

From the average to least squares

The table showed it for five candidates. Here is the claim in full, for any data at all:

Theorem. For any numbers \(b_1,\dots,b_n\), the total squared miss \[f(x) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x - b_i)^2\] is smallest at exactly one value of \(x\): the average \(\;\bar{b} = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i b_i\). Its smallest value is \(\sum_i (\bar{b} - b_i)^2\).
  • Our data: \(\bar b = \frac{19+21+20}{3} = 20\), and the leftover is \(1+1+0 = 2\) — exactly the smallest row of the table.
  • "Exactly one" is part of the claim: no tie, no second-best answer.
  • That leftover \(\sum_i(\bar b - b_i)^2\) is the error no choice of \(x\) can remove. The friends disagree with each other; no single number can fix that.
"State it as a theorem, on the board, before proving anything. Three parts: the minimizer is the average, it is unique, and the leftover has a formula." "Note what the theorem does NOT say: it says nothing about the true age. It is a statement about the numbers we were handed."

Why the average wins

You already know how to minimize a function: differentiate, set it to zero.

\[f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n (x-b_i)^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f'(x)=2\sum_{i=1}^n (x-b_i)=2\Big(nx-\sum_{i=1}^n b_i\Big)\]
  • Set \(f'(x)=0\): \(\;nx=\sum_i b_i\;\), so \(\;x=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i b_i=\bar b\). The average falls out.
  • \(f''(x)=2n>0\) everywhere, so this is a minimum — and it's the only place \(f'\) is zero, so it's the only one.
  • Our data: \(f'(x)=2(3x-60)=0 \Rightarrow x=20\).
This is exactly what training a model does: write down the error, differentiate, and go where the derivative says. Every AI system in this course is doing this — just with many variables instead of one.
"They have Calculus 2, so lead with the derivative — it is the tool they trust, and it is literally what gradient descent does." "Emphasize f'' > 0: it is what upgrades 'critical point' to 'minimum', and it is why least squares has no local-minimum trouble. That single fact is why linear regression is solvable and neural networks are not." "The homework proves the same theorem again with pure algebra. That version reveals structure the derivative hides — the error splits into a piece you control and a piece you cannot — and that split becomes the Pythagorean theorem in L03. Tell them to watch for it." "MML asserts the projection is closest (Ch 3.8, 'using geometric arguments') but never proves it. This proof is ours."

This is how AI models train

Every AI training loop does the same thing:

  1. Make a prediction.
  2. Compare it to the answer recorded in the data.
  3. Measure the error — square it, then add up over all the data.
  4. Adjust to make that total smaller.

Step 2 says recorded in the data — not the truth. A model never sees the truth. It only sees what was written down, and that can be noisy, or disagree with itself, exactly like three friends who give you three different ages.

The age problem is steps 1–3, with the three guesses as the recorded answers. Today we build the geometry of step 4.
"ChatGPT, image generators, self-driving cars — all train this way. Scale and complexity differ; the loop does not." "Be careful with step 2, and say this out loud: we are NOT comparing against truth. We compare against recorded data. A student who asks 'but how do we know the right answer?' has caught the most important thing on this slide — we don't, and we never will. Least squares needs only the recorded answers." "The math of 'adjust' is what this entire unit is about."

Name the pattern: overdetermined system

The age-guessing problem has a famous twin — the one in your pocket:

A system \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) with more equations than unknowns (\(m > n\)) is called overdetermined.

A best-fit solution minimizes \(\norm{A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}}\).
  • GPS. Each satellite reports only how far away it is — that puts you somewhere on a circle around it. Two circles already cross at a point, so with perfect readings two satellites would be enough, and a third would pass through that same point.
  • But every reading carries error, so the three circles bound a small triangle instead of meeting. No point sits on all three. The error causes that — not the extra satellite.
  • So why carry a third? Two satellites give an exact answer that is exactly as wrong as your error, with nothing to warn you. Three cannot all be satisfied — and that failure is the information.
  • \(m > n\) is not the problem; it is the redundancy that beats the noise. Your phone picks the point that misses all three by the least. AI training is the same trade: millions of examples, thousands of weights (\(m \gg n\)).
"Expect the objection, because it is the right one: three circles CAN meet at a point, so m > n does not by itself break anything. Say so plainly — with exact measurements the third satellite is redundant and consistent. Noise is what makes it inconsistent." "Then the inversion, which is the whole slide: redundancy is not the disease, it is the cure. Two satellites hand you a wrong answer with total confidence; three hand you a triangle whose size tells you how wrong you are." "If someone asks what we actually do about the noise — we do not fix the satellites. We pick the point minimising the total squared miss. That is least squares, and it is running in their pocket." "In real AI, you might have a million training examples but only 10,000 weights. That's an overdetermined system with m = 1,000,000 and n = 10,000. The math is the same as our three-friends problem, just bigger."

Quick check

Alice adds a fourth friend's guess: 20.5. Does the best-fit error go up or down compared to three friends?

Up — adding another constraint to satisfy usually increases the total error. More data means more equations to juggle, so the best compromise gets slightly worse per equation.

This is why training error in machine learning usually decreases as you add parameters but increases as you add data — more data means more constraints.
"This is a subtle but important point. More data gives a better model for new predictions (generalization), but the in-sample error on the training data goes up because there are more constraints to satisfy. We'll revisit this tradeoff in Unit 5 when we talk about regularization."

The real question: closest point

Imagine you're standing on a hill and you want to get to a road below. What's the shortest path to the road?

  • Walk straight toward the road — perpendicular.
  • Any other path would be longer.
  • The point where you hit the road is the closest point.
In math: given a point and a line (or subspace), the closest point on the line is the orthogonal projection.
"This everyday intuition — the shortest path is the perpendicular one — is the key geometric insight behind regression. If you understand this, you understand least squares."

Deriving the formula: where does the projection land?

The closest point on the line is \(\mathbf{p} = \lambda \mathbf{b}\) for some scalar \(\lambda\). Which \(\lambda\)?

Step 1: The residual must be perpendicular to the line: \[\mathbf{b}^\top \underbrace{(\mathbf{x} - \lambda \mathbf{b})}_{\text{residual}} = 0\] Step 2: Expand: \[\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{x} - \lambda\,\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{b} = 0\] Step 3: Solve for \(\lambda\): \[\boxed{\lambda = \frac{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{b}}}\]
The projection formula is not magic — it comes directly from one requirement: make the error perpendicular.
"This is a three-line derivation that every student should be able to reproduce. Start from the perpendicularity condition, expand, solve for lambda. That's it. The formula is a consequence of one geometric principle: perpendicular = closest."

Projection: a concrete example

Line direction: \(\mathbf{b} = (1,1)\). Point: \(\mathbf{x} = (2,0)\).

\[\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{b}}\,\mathbf{b} = \frac{2}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\]

Closest point on the line to \((2,0)\) is \((1,1)\).

"Let's do the calculation step by step. The numerator b^T x is the dot product we learned in Unit 1 — it measures how much x agrees with b. The denominator b^T b is the squared length of b. We divide, then scale b by that ratio. The result (1,1) is the point on the line closest to (2,0)."

The residual: what's left over

The residual is the gap between the original point and the projection:

\[\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p} = (2,0) - (1,1) = (1,-1)\]
  • The residual points from the projection back to the original point.
  • Its length \(\norm{\mathbf{r}} = \sqrt{1^2+(-1)^2} = \sqrt{2}\) is the distance from \(\mathbf{x}\) to the line.
Key property: the residual is perpendicular to the line.

Check: \(\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{b} = (1)(1) + (-1)(1) = 0\). ✓ Perpendicular!

"The residual is the error — what the line can't explain. And it's perpendicular to the line, which is exactly the hill-to-road intuition: the shortest path is the straight-down path. This perpendicularity is what makes the projection optimal."

Why perpendicular = optimal

Think about it with the Pythagorean theorem:

\[\norm{\mathbf{x}}^2 = \norm{\mathbf{p}}^2 + \norm{\mathbf{r}}^2\]
  • The projection and residual form a right triangle.
  • If the error had any component along the line, you could slide closer.
  • Perpendicular means: nothing left to improve.
"This is just the Pythagorean theorem from high school. The vector x is the hypotenuse. The projection is one leg. The residual is the other leg. Because they form a right angle, the residual is as short as possible. If you moved the projection point left or right along the line, the residual would get longer."

Proof: any other point on the line is farther

Let \(\mathbf{p} = \lambda \mathbf{b}\) be the projection and \(\mathbf{q} = \mu \mathbf{b}\) be any other point on the line (\(\mu \neq \lambda\)).

Split the distance to \(\mathbf{q}\): \[\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{q} = \underbrace{(\mathbf{x} - \lambda\mathbf{b})}_{\mathbf{r}} + \underbrace{(\lambda - \mu)\mathbf{b}}_{\text{along the line}}\] Since \(\mathbf{r} \perp \mathbf{b}\), the Pythagorean theorem gives: \[\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{q}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{r}\|^2 + (\lambda-\mu)^2\|\mathbf{b}\|^2\]
  • \(\|\mathbf{r}\|^2\) is the distance to the projection — fixed.
  • \((\lambda-\mu)^2\|\mathbf{b}\|^2 > 0\) — moving away from \(\mathbf{p}\) only adds distance.
  • Therefore \(\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{q}\| > \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}\|\). The projection is uniquely closest.
"This is the proof. It uses one fact: perpendicular vectors satisfy the Pythagorean theorem. The distance to any other point on the line equals the distance to the projection PLUS an extra positive term. So the projection is strictly the closest. Three lines of algebra, one big idea."

Quick check

If \(\mathbf{x}\) already lies on the line through \(\mathbf{b}\), what is the projection of \(\mathbf{x}\)?

\(\mathbf{x}\) itself — it's already on the line, so the closest point is itself. The residual is zero.

In AI terms: if the model already perfectly predicts the data, the error is zero and there's nothing to improve.

The projection formula

Onto a line (1D subspace \(U = \text{span}\{\mathbf{b}\}\)): \[\pi_U(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{b}}\,\mathbf{b}\] Onto a subspace (with basis columns \(B\)): \[\pi_U(\mathbf{x}) = B(B^\top B)^{-1}B^\top \mathbf{x}\]
  • The line formula uses the dot product from Unit 1.
  • The subspace formula uses matrix multiplication from Unit 1.
  • Three properties: (1) result lies in the subspace, (2) it's unique, (3) residual is perpendicular.
"Notice how everything builds on Unit 1. The dot product in the numerator measures agreement. The matrix multiplication in the general formula is the multi-dimensional version. Units in this course are like Lego blocks — each one snaps onto the previous."

The big connection: least squares = projection

Minimizing \(\norm{A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}}\) is the same as projecting \(\mathbf{b}\) onto the column space of \(A\).
  • \(A\mathbf{x}\) lives in the column space of \(A\) — the set of all outputs the system can produce.
  • The best-fit \(\mathbf{x}^*\) makes \(A\mathbf{x}^*\) the closest point in that column space to \(\mathbf{b}\).
  • The residual \(\mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x}^*\) is perpendicular to the column space.
This one idea — best fit is a projection — is the geometric foundation of all linear AI models.
"This is the single most important idea in this unit. When you train a linear model, you're projecting the true labels onto the space of things the model can produce. The best model is the closest point. And 'closest' means perpendicular residual. Everything else in this unit is just computing that projection."

Example: fitting a line through data

Three data points: \((0, 1)\), \((1, 2)\), \((2, 2)\). We want the best line \(\hat{y} = w_0 + w_1 t\).

\[A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\] Each row: (1, \(t_i\)). The "1" column handles the intercept \(w_0\).
  • The columns of \(A\) span a plane in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).
  • We want the point on that plane closest to \(\mathbf{b}\).
  • That's a projection — exactly what we just learned!
"Each data point gives one equation. Three points, two unknowns (slope and intercept). The system is overdetermined. The best line is the projection of b onto the column space of A. We'll compute this with the normal equation in L04."

Quick check

If all three points lie exactly on a line, what is the least-squares residual?

Zero — \(\mathbf{b}\) is already in the column space of \(A\), so the "closest point" is \(\mathbf{b}\) itself.

L03 takeaways

  • Overdetermined systems have more equations than unknowns — no exact answer. [MASTER]
  • Projection gives the closest point; the residual is perpendicular. [MASTER]
  • Projection formula onto a line: \(\lambda = \frac{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{b}}\). [MASTER]
  • Subspace projection formula: \(B(B^\top B)^{-1}B^\top\). [UNDERSTAND]
Next lecture: we'll use this geometry to derive a formula for the best-fit line. That formula is how every linear regression model works.

L04 — Predicting apartment rent

You're apartment hunting. You notice: bigger apartments cost more, and farther from campus costs less.

  • Size: 800, 600, 1000 sqft.
  • Distance to campus: 1, 2, 0.5 miles.
  • Rent: $3000, $2000, $5000.
Can we find a formula that predicts rent from size and distance? What line (or plane) fits best?
"Everyone's dealt with apartment hunting — or at least compared prices online. Today we'll build the exact formula that Zillow, Redfin, and every real estate app uses to estimate prices. It's called linear regression, and it's the same projection from L03, just written as a formula."

Your data is a matrix

Organize the apartment data into a matrix:

\[X = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 1 \\ 6 & 2 \\ 10 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{(features: size in 100s, distance)}\] \[\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{(rent in $1000s)}\]
  • Each row = one apartment (one data point).
  • Each column = one feature.
  • This is the feature matrix from Unit 1 — now we'll use it to predict.
"Remember from Unit 1: a matrix is a grid of numbers. Here each row is an apartment, each column is a feature. We want to find weights w1 and w2 such that w1 × size + w2 × distance ≈ rent."

Prediction = matrix × weights

Try weights \(\mathbf{w} = (0.4, -0.6)^\top\): "each 100 sqft adds $400, each mile subtracts $600."

\[\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 1 \\ 6 & 2 \\ 10 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0.4 \\ -0.6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.6 \\ 1.2 \\ 3.7 \end{bmatrix}\]
  • Each prediction is a dot product: \(\hat{y}_i = \mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{w}\).
  • Predictions: $2600, $1200, $3700. True rents: $3000, $2000, $5000.
  • Not great — but how bad exactly?
"This is matrix-vector multiplication from Unit 1 in action. Each prediction is one row of X dotted with w. The predictions are off — we need a single number to measure 'how off', and then find the w that makes that number as small as possible."

The intercept trick

The formula \(\hat{y} = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2\) forces the line through the origin. To add a baseline rent \(w_0\):

Add a column of 1s to \(X\): \[\tilde{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 8 & 1 \\ 1 & 6 & 2 \\ 1 & 10 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} w_0 \\ w_1 \\ w_2 \end{bmatrix}\] Now \(\hat{y}_i = w_0 + w_1 x_{i1} + w_2 x_{i2}\) — intercept included automatically.
Every real model uses this trick. The "1" column is a fake feature that's always on, so its weight becomes the baseline.
"This is a very common trick. Rather than treating the intercept specially, we add a fake feature that's always 1. Then the weight for that column is the intercept. Everything stays as matrix multiplication — clean and uniform."

Mean Squared Error: one number for "how bad"

True rents: \(\mathbf{y} = (3, 2, 5)\). Predictions: \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = (2.6, 1.2, 3.7)\).

ApartmentTruePredictedErrorSquared error
A3.02.60.40.16
B2.01.20.80.64
C5.03.71.31.69
\[\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 = \frac{0.16+0.64+1.69}{3} = 0.83\]
MSE = one number that lets us compare any two models: lower is better.
"MSE is the report card for your model. Just like your GPA is one number that summarizes your academic performance, MSE is one number that summarizes how well your model predicts. The goal of training is to make this number as small as possible."

MSE in matrix notation

\[J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{n}\norm{X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{w})^2\]
  • \(X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\) is the residual vector from L03.
  • \(\norm{\cdot}^2\) is the squared norm from Unit 1.
  • Everything connects: dot product → norm → residual → MSE.
The entire vocabulary of Unit 1 (vectors, norms, dot products) was preparation for this moment: measuring model error.
"See how everything fits together? The dot product computes each prediction. The norm measures the total error. The residual is the gap between prediction and truth. MSE is just the squared norm of the residual, divided by n. This is not a coincidence — we designed the course this way."

Quick check

If one residual doubles (say apartment B's error goes from 0.8 to 1.6), how does its squared contribution change?

It quadruples — from 0.64 to 2.56. Squared error amplifies large mistakes.

This is why AI models care more about big errors than small ones. A prediction that's off by 10 counts 100× as much as one that's off by 1.

Wait — we've seen this before!

Minimize \(\norm{X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}}\) — this is exactly the least squares problem from L03!
  • \(X\mathbf{w}^*\) is the projection of \(\mathbf{y}\) onto the column space of \(X\).
  • The residual \(\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}^*\) is perpendicular to the column space.
  • The best weights \(\mathbf{w}^*\) are the coordinates of that projection.
The best-fit line isn't arbitrary — it's the unique prediction that is geometrically closest to the true data.
"This is the payoff from L03. We learned that the closest point is a projection, and the residual is perpendicular. Now we're applying the same idea: the best linear model is the one whose predictions are the projection of the true labels onto the model's output space."

Worked example: the simplest model

The simplest model predicts the same number for everything: \(\hat{y} = w\) (constant).

\[X = \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\3\\5\end{bmatrix}\]

The column space of \(X\) is the line \((c, c, c)\). We're projecting \(\mathbf{y}\) onto that line.

Best \(w\): \(\displaystyle w^* = \frac{X^\top \mathbf{y}}{X^\top X} = \frac{2+3+5}{3} = \frac{10}{3} \approx 3.33\).

The constant best-fit model predicts the mean! Our age-guessing problem returns.
"Full circle: the average from the beginning of this lecture is just the simplest regression. A constant model has one parameter, and the best constant is the mean. Linear regression with a slope and intercept is the same idea but with more parameters."

Deriving the normal equation

We know from L03: the best fit makes the residual perpendicular to the column space. Let's turn this into a formula.

Step 1 — State the goal: the residual \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}\) must be perpendicular to every column of \(X\).

Step 2 — Write it in math: "\(\mathbf{r}\) is perpendicular to every column" means: \[X^\top \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{0}\] (Each row of \(X^\top\) dots with \(\mathbf{r}\) and gives zero.)

Step 3 — Substitute \(\mathbf{r}\): \[X^\top(\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{0}\]
Step 4 — Expand and rearrange: \[X^\top \mathbf{y} - X^\top X \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{X^\top X \,\mathbf{w} = X^\top \mathbf{y}}\]
Four steps: goal → math → substitute → rearrange. The normal equation is a direct consequence of perpendicularity.
"This is the same three-line derivation as the projection formula from L03, just in matrix form. Step 1: state the geometric principle (perpendicular). Step 2: write it as an equation. Step 3-4: do the algebra. I want every student to be able to reproduce this on the board."

The normal equation: summary

\[\boxed{X^\top X \,\mathbf{w} = X^\top \mathbf{y}}\]
  • Where it comes from: residual ⊥ column space (the projection principle).
  • What it does: finds the weights that minimize squared error.
  • If \(X^\top X\) is invertible: \(\mathbf{w}^* = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top \mathbf{y}\).
  • The word "normal" means perpendicular (not "ordinary").
This is the same idea as the 1D projection formula \(\lambda = \frac{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{b}}\), generalized to multiple dimensions.
"Compare: in 1D, b^T(x - λb) = 0 gave us λ = b^T x / b^T b. In multiple dimensions, X^T(y - Xw) = 0 gives us X^T X w = X^T y. Same idea, bigger matrices."

Worked example: solve the normal equation by hand

Model: \(\hat{y} = ax\) (no intercept). Data: \(x = (1,2,3)\), \(y = (2,2,3)\).

\[X^\top X = 1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2 = 14\] \[X^\top \mathbf{y} = 1\cdot 2 + 2\cdot 2 + 3\cdot 3 = 15\] \[a^* = \frac{15}{14} \approx 1.07\]

Best-fit line: \(\hat{y} = 1.07x\).

You just did what sklearn.linear_model.LinearRegression does internally — with pencil and paper!
"This is a one-parameter problem so the normal equation is just division. For two parameters you solve a 2×2 system. For 100 features it's a 100×100 system — that's where computers help. But the math is the same."

Quick check

If \(X^\top X\) is not invertible, what does that suggest about the columns of \(X\)?

The columns are linearly dependent — some features are redundant. For example, if one column is twice another, you can't tell which feature is "doing the work."

Real-world example: if you include both temperature in °C and °F as features, one is redundant. The formula can't separate their contributions.

L04 takeaways

  • Regression predicts with \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\mathbf{w}\) — matrix × vector. [MASTER]
  • MSE is the one number that defines "best fit." [MASTER]
  • The normal equation \(X^\top X\mathbf{w} = X^\top \mathbf{y}\) — a direct formula. [MASTER]
  • Solve by hand for small examples (1-2 parameters). [MASTER]
But the normal equation has limits: it requires computing \(X^\top X\) and inverting it. What if that matrix is too big, or nearly singular? Next lectures: we'll explore when things go wrong — and what to do about it.

L05 — Can you take all the classes you want?

You want to take 4 classes, but some meet at the same time. Can you find a schedule that satisfies every constraint?

  • Each constraint: "this class needs this time slot."
  • Sometimes all constraints can be satisfied (exact solution).
  • Sometimes some conflict — you have to compromise.
Linear systems are the math of constraints. Today: when do exact solutions exist, and what structure tells us?
"Everyone's experienced this: you want certain classes, but time slots conflict. A linear system is the mathematical version of this problem. Each equation is a constraint. We want to know: can all constraints be satisfied simultaneously? If not, what's the best we can do? We already know the 'best we can do' part from L03–L04. Today we focus on when exact solutions exist."

Example: two equations, two unknowns

You and a friend are splitting a restaurant bill. You ordered differently:

  • You: 1 taco + 1 drink = $5
  • Friend: 1 taco − 1 drink = $1 (friend paid less for just a taco)
\[\begin{cases} x + y = 5 \\ x - y = 1 \end{cases}\] Add: \(2x = 6 \Rightarrow x = 3\). Substitute: \(y = 2\).

Taco costs $3, drink costs $2. One exact solution.

"Two equations, two unknowns — and they agree on one answer. But what if the equations conflict? That's the question we'll explore."

Three possible outcomes for a linear system

A linear system \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) can have:
  1. Exactly one solution — lines cross at one point.
  2. No solution — parallel lines, contradictory constraints.
  3. Infinitely many — same line, redundant constraints.
"In 2D, these correspond to lines crossing, lines being parallel, or lines being the same. In higher dimensions, the geometry is harder to visualize but the three cases still apply."

Quick check

What if the second equation were \(x + y = 10\) instead of \(x - y = 1\)?
\(\begin{cases} x + y = 5 \\ x + y = 10 \end{cases}\)

No solution — \(x + y\) can't be both 5 and 10. The equations are contradictory (parallel lines).

Column space: what can the model produce?

Think of a vending machine with two buttons. Button A dispenses a combo of items, Button B dispenses a different combo. You can press each button any number of times.

  • The column space is everything you can get from combining button presses.
  • If what you want is in the column space → exact solution exists.
  • If it's outside the column space → no exact solution.
The column space of \(A\): \(\text{col}(A) = \{A\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n\}\) — all possible outputs.

\(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) has a solution \(\iff\) \(\mathbf{b} \in \text{col}(A)\).
"The column space is all the targets the system can hit. Think of it as the menu of the vending machine. If what you want is on the menu, great. If not, the best you can do is get the closest available item — and that's the projection from L03."

Example: what can this matrix produce?

\[A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\] Any \(A\mathbf{x}\) has the form \(\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\0\end{pmatrix}\).
  • The column space is a plane in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) — the \(xy\)-plane.
  • Can we hit \(\mathbf{b} = (1, 2, 3)^\top\)? No — the third entry must be 0.
  • Can we hit \(\mathbf{b} = (1, 2, 0)^\top\)? Yes — \(\mathbf{x} = (1, 2)^\top\).
In AI: the column space tells you what your model can predict. A linear model can't produce curved predictions — that's outside its column space.
"This matrix can only produce vectors with a zero in the third position. It's like a model that can't predict certain patterns — they're outside its capabilities. When the target is outside the column space, we project — which is exactly regression."

Rank: how many independent directions?

The rank of \(A\) = dimension of its column space = number of independent columns.
  • Rank 1: the model can only produce multiples of one direction.
  • Rank 2: the model spans a plane.
  • Rank = number of columns: the model is as flexible as possible (full rank).
More independent features = higher rank = more flexible model. But too much flexibility leads to overfitting — a topic for Unit 5.
"Rank is like the degrees of freedom of your model. A rank-1 model can only scale one pattern. A rank-2 model can mix two patterns. Ideally you have as many independent patterns as you need to explain the data — but not more."

Quick check

If every row of \(A\) has last entry zero, what must be true about \(\mathbf{b}\) for \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) to be solvable?

The last entry of \(\mathbf{b}\) must be zero — because \(A\mathbf{x}\) can never produce anything else in that position.

Overdetermined systems: putting it together

Back to the age-guessing problem: \(x = 19, x = 21, x = 20\).

\[A = \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix}19\\21\\20\end{bmatrix}\] Column space = the line \((c, c, c)\). But \(\mathbf{b} = (19, 21, 20)\) is NOT on that line.
  • \(\mathbf{b}\) is outside the column space → no exact solution.
  • Best fit: project \(\mathbf{b}\) onto the column space → get \((20, 20, 20)\).
  • This is the normal equation from L04: \(x^* = 20\) (the mean).
Column space + projection = the complete picture of least squares.
"Now we have the full story. The column space tells you what's reachable. If the target isn't reachable, project. The projection gives the best fit. The normal equation computes it. Everything from L03 and L04 snaps together."

The normal equation means: residual ⊥ column space

\[A^\top(A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{0}\] This says: the residual \(\mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x}\) is perpendicular to every column of \(A\).
  • The residual has no component the model could explain.
  • Everything the model can do, it has done.
  • The leftover is pure noise — perpendicular to the model's reach.
"This is a beautiful result. The normal equation isn't just a formula — it's a geometric statement. It says: the best model uses up all its explanatory power. Whatever's left over is noise that no linear model can reduce."

Quick check

If \(\mathbf{b}\) is already in the column space of \(A\), what is the residual at the normal-equation solution?

Zero — \(\mathbf{b}\) is already on the plane, so no perpendicular drop is needed.

L05 takeaways

  • Linear systems: three outcomes (one, none, infinitely many solutions). [MASTER]
  • Column space determines which targets are reachable. [UNDERSTAND]
  • Rank = number of independent columns = flexibility of the model. [UNDERSTAND]
Next: what happens when the normal equation exists but gives unreliable answers? That's the stability problem, and we'll need the pseudoinverse to handle it.

L06 — When the formula gives garbage

You've seen autocorrect go wildly wrong — you type one wrong letter and get a completely different word.

  • A small change in input → a huge change in output.
  • The same thing can happen with the normal equation.
  • Tiny noise in the data → wildly different best-fit weights.
When can we trust the formula? And what do we do when we can't?
"Everyone's had an autocorrect fail where one typo completely changes the message. The same thing happens in math: some formulas are 'sensitive' — small changes in the input cause wild swings in the output. Today we'll see when the normal equation has this problem and what to do about it."

What if the matrix isn't square?

We can only invert square matrices. But data matrices are usually tall (more rows than columns):

\[X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{(3 data points, 1 parameter)}\] \(X\) is 3×1 — no inverse exists. But we still want to "undo" \(X\) to find the best weights.
The pseudoinverse \(A^+\) acts like an inverse for non-square matrices. It gives the best-fit solution.
"Regular inverses only exist for square matrices. But most data matrices aren't square — you have more data points than parameters. The pseudoinverse is a 'best-effort' inverse that handles this case. For our purposes, you can think of it as the formula that the computer uses internally when you call a regression function."

Example: pseudoinverse in action

Data: \(x = (1, 2, 3)\), \(y = (1, 2, 2.5)\). Model: \(\hat{y} = ax\).

\[A = \begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\2.5\end{bmatrix}\] \[A^\top A = 1+4+9 = 14\] \[A^\top \mathbf{b} = 1+4+7.5 = 12.5\] \[a^* = \frac{12.5}{14} \approx 0.893\]

Best-fit line: \(\hat{y} = 0.893x\). The pseudoinverse produced this: \(A^+ = \frac{A^\top}{A^\top A}\).

"For a single-column matrix, the pseudoinverse is just A^T divided by A^T A. For larger matrices, computers use more sophisticated methods (like SVD), but the result is the same: the least-squares solution."

The pseudoinverse: key properties

The pseudoinverse \(A^+\) gives: \[\mathbf{x}^* = A^+\mathbf{b}\]
  • If \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) has a solution → \(A^+\mathbf{b}\) finds it.
  • If no exact solution → \(A^+\mathbf{b}\) gives the least-squares fit.
  • If multiple solutions → \(A^+\mathbf{b}\) picks the one with smallest norm.
When \(A\) is square and invertible: \(A^+ = A^{-1}\). The pseudoinverse is a generalization of the regular inverse.
"The pseudoinverse handles every case: exact solutions, overdetermined systems, underdetermined systems. It's the Swiss Army knife of linear algebra. In Python, numpy.linalg.pinv computes it for you."

When many solutions exist: pick the simplest

Sometimes a system has infinitely many solutions:

\[A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\\1 & 0\\1 & 0\end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\2\\2\end{bmatrix}\] Any \(\mathbf{x} = (2, t)^\top\) works for any \(t\). Which one to pick?
  • The pseudoinverse picks \(t = 0\): \(\mathbf{x}^* = (2, 0)^\top\).
  • This has the smallest length — the simplest solution.
Prefer simplicity when accuracy is tied. This is a preview of regularization (Unit 5).
"When many solutions give the same error, pick the simplest one. 'Simplest' means smallest parameter values — don't use a complicated model when a simple one works equally well. This idea will come back as regularization in Unit 5, where we'll deliberately penalize complexity."

Quick check

If \(\mathbf{b}\) lies in the column space of \(A\), what does \(A^+\mathbf{b}\) give?

An exact solution — no approximation needed. The pseudoinverse reduces to the regular inverse (or finds one of the exact solutions, the one with minimum norm).

The stability problem

Consider two features that are almost identical:

\[A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.001 \end{bmatrix}\] The columns are 99.9% the same. What happens?
  • \(A^\top A\) is nearly singular — almost has determinant zero.
  • Change one data point by 0.001 → the solution moves 1000× as far. Concretely: \(\mathbf{x}\) goes from \((1,1)\) to \((0,2)\), a shift of 1 in each entry from a nudge of 0.001.
  • The formula is correct but unreliable.
A correct formula can still give garbage if the inputs are nearly dependent.
"This is like weighing yourself on a scale that changes by 10 pounds every time you shift your foot slightly. The scale is correct — it's just amplifying tiny differences. The same thing happens when your features are almost identical: the formula amplifies noise instead of finding a stable answer."

Real-world instability

Imagine predicting house prices using:

  • Feature 1: temperature in °C
  • Feature 2: temperature in °F

These are linearly related: \(F = 1.8C + 32\). The columns of \(X\) are almost proportional.

Result: the normal equation produces weights like \(w_1 = +500, w_2 = -499\). The individual weights are meaningless — only their combination matters. Tiny noise → wildly different \(w_1, w_2\) values.
"This is called multicollinearity in statistics. It doesn't mean the prediction is bad — it means the individual weights can't be trusted. The model can't tell which feature is doing the work because they carry almost the same information."

Condition number: how fragile is the system?

The condition number \(\kappa(A)\) measures how much small changes in data get amplified in the solution.
  • \(\kappa \approx 1\): stable — small changes in data → small changes in solution.
  • \(\kappa \gg 1\): unstable — small changes in data → huge changes in solution.
  • Our \(A = \begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1.001\end{bmatrix}\) has \(\kappa \approx 4000\).
  • An error of 0.001 in the data could cause an error of \(0.001 \times 4000 = 4\) in the solution.
In Python: np.linalg.cond(A) tells you if you should worry. Condition number > 1000? Be cautious.
"Think of the condition number as a sensitivity dial. Low condition number means the formula is trustworthy. High condition number means proceed with caution — the answer might change a lot if you measure slightly differently."

Three fixes for instability

  1. Remove redundant features — if two columns are nearly identical, drop one.
    Check with np.corrcoef() — correlation near ±1 means redundancy.
  2. Use better algorithms — QR decomposition instead of forming \(X^\top X\).
    This is what np.linalg.lstsq does internally — more numerically stable.
  3. Add regularization — penalize large weights to keep them stable.
    Coming in Unit 5: Ridge and Lasso regression.
The normal equation is the starting point, not the final tool. Real AI uses smarter methods — but they all solve the same projection problem.
"You won't need to implement QR decomposition by hand. But you should know it exists, and you should always check the condition number before trusting the normal equation. In practice, sklearn handles this for you — but understanding why it matters makes you a better data scientist."

Quick check

If two columns of \(A\) are exactly identical (not just close), what happens to \(A^\top A\)?

It becomes singular — determinant is zero, no inverse exists. The normal equation has no unique solution.

This is the extreme case of instability. Nearly identical = unstable. Exactly identical = formula doesn't work at all. The pseudoinverse still gives a solution (minimum norm), but the weights are not interpretable.

L06 takeaways

  • The pseudoinverse generalizes the inverse for non-square matrices. [UNDERSTAND]
  • Stability matters: nearly dependent features amplify noise. [UNDERSTAND]
  • The condition number tells you how fragile the system is. [UNDERSTAND]
  • Three fixes: remove redundancy, better algorithms, regularization. [UNDERSTAND]
Preview: gradient descent (Unit 4) avoids forming \(X^\top X\) entirely. Regularization (Unit 5) tames instability by penalizing complexity. The math you've built in this unit is the foundation for both.
